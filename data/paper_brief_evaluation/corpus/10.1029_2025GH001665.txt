Abstract

West Nile Virus (WNV) is the leading cause of mosquito‐borne disease in the United States, yet predicting its transmission remains a significant challenge for public health officials. Many studies use correlative statistical models to predict disease cases using temperature and humidity, but accessible mechanistic approaches that simulate mosquito behavior traits can capture more of the transmission process. This study adapts an existing R‐language package, the Mosquito‐borne Viral Suitability Estimator (MVSE), which was created to model Aedes aegypti mosquito transmission of dengue, and tunes it to Culex pipiens transmission of WNV. Equations for mortality, biting rate, and incubation period were updated, and Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD) was used in place of Relative Humidity to better approximate dehydration stress on mosquitos. Our Culex‐specific customizations of the model improved correlations with annual disease cases at the county level, particularly in locations with higher Culex pipiens habitat suitability. For West Nile Neuroinvasive Disease (WNND), the correlation between MVSE performance (Spearman's rho for WNND incidence and our custom Index P) and probability of Culex pipiens presence was 0.48 (p = 0.03). These results demonstrate that incorporating trait sensitivity to temperature and VPD results in species‐specific models that may be effective in informing WNV disease prevention efforts.

Plain Language Summary

West Nile virus disease is a serious health threat in the United States that can cause severe brain inflammation in extreme (neuroinvasive) cases and has historically been difficult to predict. How mosquitos spread it from birds to people is complex. Weather conditions like temperature and humidity affect the behavior of mosquitos. However, many computer models used to predict outbreaks do not account for the unique ways different mosquito species react to these environmental cues. We updated an existing model framework that was originally created for a different mosquito and disease to specifically reflect how the Culex pipiens mosquito—a prime carrier of WNV—survives and transmits the virus. When we tested this custom model against the original version in 20 U.S. cities with high disease incidence, our updated version performed better in locations that had more Culex pipiens mosquitos. This shows that fine‐tuning models to the specific traits of the local mosquito can lead to more accurate predictions, which helps public health officials take steps to better protect communities.

Key Points

Transmission suitability indices for arboviruses can be well‐correlated with disease cases when tuned to specific mosquito species traits

Culex pipiens‐specific traits like seasonal diapause and sensitivity to humidity are important factors to represent in model logic

Model improvements were most apparent at the county‐annual scale, but model performance was best at the state‐monthly scale

1 Introduction

1.1 Challenges of Skillful Prediction of West Nile Virus Disease Cases

West Nile Virus (WNV) is the largest cause of domestic arboviral disease (Sutter et al., 2024). Since its introduction to the United States in 1999, WNV disease (WND) has caused at least 61,013 human disease cases, 29,077 hospitalizations (since 2004), and 3,144 deaths through 2024 as of 16 January 2026 according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (2026). There are two types of WND cases, with roughly half of those reported being of the milder West Nile Fever (WNF) form and half being the severe neuroinvasive (WNND) form. It is widely held that cases of WNF are far more prevalent but underreported compared to the severe WNND cases because WNF often manifests as a febrile illness common to many other maladies, while the severity of WNND drives patients to seek treatment (Petersen et al., 2013). WND deaths are primarily from WNND (U.S. CDC, 2026).

Improving the ability of health authorities to predict when and where outbreaks of WNV disease (WND) disease may occur to support prevention and control is a strategic priority of the CDC (U.S. DHHS & CDC, 2024). While many modeling teams have attempted to forecast disease cases using environmental, sociodemographic, and other variables, case counts have exhibited great interannual and spatial variability that has often defied skillful prediction. An analysis of CDC's 2020 WNV Forecasting Challenge found that no models at the regional (or even smaller ∼34,640 km2 hexagon) scale significantly outperformed a simple negative binomial model in forecasting challenges (Holcomb et al., 2023). In the 2022 WNV Forecasting Challenge, models had improved overall, with the ensemble outperforming the simple model. However, more complex models using climate and other factors had less skill than regression‐based models (Harp et al., 2025).

Retrospective regression analyses of WNF and WNND cases via correlative statistical models have revealed similar challenges in predicting disease outcomes. Studies have generally identified positive associations between temperature and WNV disease cases, while findings for precipitation and humidity are variable (Davis et al., 2018; Karki et al., 2020; Soverow et al., 2009). Coarse correlative models mask complex interactions between disease vectors, hosts, and the environment. Many vector behavior and life history traits, such as biting rate and vector competence, are maximized at an optimal temperature and humidity level (Brown et al., 2023; Di Pol et al., 2022). This complicates what can be understood from statistical modeling that does not explicitly represent these relationships. For example, Ukawuba and Shaman (2018) report that increased specific humidity in June in Texas increases the risk of WNV transmission, whereas further increases in temperature and specific humidity in July and August decreased risk of WNV transmission. Hess et al. (2018) also report on the relative importance of temperature, humidity, and precipitation at different times of year.

A relatively new class of ento‐epidemiological models attempts to predict R0, the basic reproduction number, as a transmission index driven by temperature. Compared to correlative statistical approaches, these models more directly simulate the effect of temperature on mosquito behavioral traits such as biting rate and mortality rate and are thus better able to represent their unimodal relationship with temperature. For example, Muñoz et al. (2020) built an ensemble of four of these models to predict the overall environmental suitability for Aedes‐borne diseases from air temperatures. While these models do not predict disease cases as outcomes, by mechanistically simulating environmental suitability for transmission they may outperform correlative models in representing how changes in temperature influence disease case outcomes. Mordecai et al. (2017) showed that temperature‐driven R0 models effectively predicted incidence of dengue, chikungunya, and Zika viruses.

There are several barriers preventing wider use of ento‐epidemiological models for predicting WNV disease. Existing literature has often focused on Aedes‐vectored diseases. The corresponding model code is often not packaged for accessibility and reuse. The transmission pathway for WND is complicated by the involvement of avian amplifying hosts that extend the effective range and rate of spread of the disease. Birds also serve as reservoirs that experience high viremia, which could dampen the relative importance of environmental suitability in predicting overall disease cases. Finally, laboratory and field data on Culex species that inform mechanistic models simulating transmission is limited.

1.2 Tailoring an Existing Ento‐Epidemiological Model for Culex Transmission Modeling

In 2019, Obolski et al. published an accessible R‐language package, Mosquito‐borne Viral Suitability Estimator (MVSE) that allows users to estimate a transmission suitability index, IndexP, that is simplified from an R0 model and is tuned to Aedes mosquitos. MVSE applies established temperature‐ and humidity‐based formulas for mosquito traits and can be adapted to estimate transmission suitability for other disease vectors, such as Culex mosquitos. Studies focused on WND in Israel (Lourenço et al., 2020) and Portugal (Lourenço et al., 2022) have shown promising results by driving MVSE with user‐specified parameters, but they did not adapt the underlying model to use Culex‐specific models of trait sensitivity to environmental parameters.

In 2023, Nakase et al., published an update to MVSE with source code for the package shared on GitHub. The new package included a variety of updates to the functions that estimate mosquito traits from temperature and humidity—but the model remained tuned to Aedes mosquito traits. Given the demonstrated success by Lourenço et al. (2020), Lourenço et al. (2022) in using this package that was tuned for Aedes mosquitos in predicting transmission for Culex‐vectored diseases, we hypothesize that reformulating the internal weather‐dependent functions in MVSE to fit Culex mosquito dynamics using newly available modeling and laboratory studies will improve the correlation between Index P and cases of WND. We focus our study on the United States because MVSE has not yet been applied to studying WNV there, and because robust data on both WND and WNND are available from CDC's ArboNet to correlate with Index P.

In this study, we modify the MVSE package to update the formulas used to calculate Culex adult mosquito lifespan (1μ(u,t)v), biting rate (a(u,t)v), and Extrinsic Incubation Period (EIP) (γ(t)v) with Culex‐specific formulations. We also introduce a new approach to incorporating the influence of humidity that is based on vapor pressure deficit (VPD) rather than relative humidity (RH) that was informed by Brown et al. (2023). We then use weather station observations from the 20 U.S. counties with the largest case counts of WND and WNND to retrospectively estimate Index P using both the old and new formulations of the package. Finally we correlate the transmission indices with common forecasting targets, observed incidence of WNND and WNF, to determine if the updates to the model could improve predictions of WND cases. The reporting of this modeling study was structured to follow recommended reporting items for epidemic forecasting and prediction research (EPIFORGE) guidelines (Pollett et al., 2021).

2 Materials and Methods

2.1 Updates to Index P Trait Models

The theory and methodological details for Index P were first described in Obolski et al. (2019) and later updated with the release of a new version of the MVSE package detailed in Nakase et al. (2023). In this study, we report on further updates to the theory and methodological details of the MVSE package to improve its utility in modeling transmission potential for Culex pipiens mosquitos and the diseases they transmit, such as WNV disease. We chose to focus on Cx. pipiens specifically because it is one of the most common vectors for WNV (Ciota et al., 2013) and because there were several studies examining sensitivity of life history traits to temperature and humidity that we could use to update the MVSE package. We update the equations used to fit vector‐specific variables (Table 1) to Culex‐appropriate versions: biting rate is reformulated to also be sensitive to temperature a(u)v→a(u,t)v and to use VPD instead of RH; mortality rate (μ(u,t)v) is also reformulated to use VPD, and EIP (1/γ(t)v) is updated based on Culex pipiens studies. The probability of transmission from host to vector (ϕ(t)h→v) and transmission from vector to host (ϕ(t)v→h) are not modified in this study because the package by Nakase et al. (2023) is based on work by Lambrechts et al. (2011) whose vector competence models were based on empirical data from studies on WNV and other encephalitis‐inducing viruses, so they are well (perhaps even better) suited to a Culex‐tuned model.

2.2 Relative Humidity (RH) Versus Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD)

Relative humidity is likely the most commonly measure of the concentration of water vapor in air for disease modeling studies. It is a widely reported variable, readily available in many meteorological and climatological data sets. It represents the ratio of water vapor pressure in ambient air (i.e., the pressure exerted by water vapor molecules as a function of temperature and density) to the vapor pressure at full saturation. It is an indication of how much moisture is in the air relative to how much could be held by the air (Davis et al., 2016). A significant, and often unstated and violated, assumption made when using RH is that temperature is held constant. Saturation vapor pressure is only sensitive to temperature, but the vapor pressure of air is sensitive to both temperature and the density of water vapor (absolute humidity). If a parcel of dry air were to be rapidly cooled, its RH would increase even though the moisture content of the air is the same. This means that RH cannot be interpreted without considering temperature, and the sensitivity of RH to temperature means that it also exhibits temperature‐driven swings diurnally and seasonally (Davis et al., 2016).

Mosquitos are not sensitive to RH, per se; they are sensitive to vapor pressure. Mosquitos are under near constant dehydration stress from the environment (evaporation), and they must alter their behavior to maintain homeostasis to function properly (Holmes & Benoit, 2019). While differences in RH can reasonably approximate differences in vapor pressure under constant temperature, studies that examine mosquito behavior in time or in space (nearly all), violate this assumption of constant temperature. Brown et al. (2023) posit a better variable for use in such studies that more directly represents the evaporative stress faced by mosquitos: Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD). This variable is closely related to RH, but its derivation marginalizes temperature to focus on how differences in the density of water vapor between the air that was measured versus that of the density of fully saturated air. The higher the VPD, the faster the rate of water loss to the environment (Holmes & Benoit, 2019) and thus the more dehydration stress experienced by a mosquito.

Fortunately, VPD is easy to calculate given RH and T, and we use the Tetens approximation for saturation vapor pressure in this study (Monteith & Unsworth, 2013). We calculate VPD from daily mean RH and mean temperature because these variables are already in use in both versions of the package. While use of daily max and min temperature to calculate VPD rather than mean temperature is the preferred method, since we are further scaling the effect of VPD on mosquito behavior traits, the use of mean temperature should be adequate for our purposes. Unfortunately, as Brown et al. (2023) point out, studies in which careful joint consideration of the interaction between temperature and humidity are few, and effectively incorporating a humidity variable (either RH or VPD) into a mechanistic model is still challenged by a lack of empirical studies. In MVSE, while many behavioral traits are modeled explicitly using temperature‐based equations, the application of RH is indirectly applied. RH (u) is first normalized to a value between zero and one and then its effect is multiplied with the effect of temperature (in the case of mosquito mortality). The effects of temperature and humidity on mortality (μˇ(t)v and μˇ(u)v) are modified by fitting scaling coefficients (using MCMC) so that the posterior distribution of each roughly approximates the specified distribution. This fitting process is further detailed in Nakase et al. (2023). We continue to use this approach (Equation 1), but we replace RH with VPD. This is a fair substitute because both variables are aiming to measure evaporative stress on mosquitos, but we must flip the sign with VPD to maintain meaning since an increase in RH (assuming constant temperature) would decrease stress, but an increase in VPD increases stress. Because empirical laboratory studies that explicitly quantify the functional response of Culex life‐history traits to Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD) are currently unavailable in the literature, we maintained the theoretical scaling approach from Nakase et al. (2023), but swap in VPD for RH given the rationale described above and in Brown et al. (2023). This approach is also used in the original MVSE model and is described further in Lourenço and Recker (2014). While this is a demonstrated theoretical framework for estimating the effect of a humidity parameter on mosquito traits, future studies should be done to validate it with empirical data. 2.5 Incubation Period

In the original MVSE package by Obolski et al. (2019), the EIP is modeled as a function of temperature and scaled using a coefficient (α) that is set using MCMC in the same way that mortality is scaled using (η). Nakase et al. (2023) dispense with the scaling coefficient and instead adopt a series of lognormally distributed random variables that represent estimated EIP at different temperature ranges based on an empirical study by Chan and Johansson (2012). We adopt the latter approach, taking advantage of a new study by Vollans et al. (2024) where the empirical data for EIP in Cx. pipiens from Kilpatrick et al. (2008) was reanalyzed using Bayesian time delay models to produce Weibull distributed estimates of the effect of temperature on EIP. We modified the MVSE package to accept Weibull distributed data and coded pre‐defined distributions at set temperature thresholds ranging from 15°C to 50°C, using the moment matching approach from Justus et al. (1978) to estimate the parameters of the distribution from the moments provided in Vollans et al. (2024). 2.6 Analysis of Original and Modified MVSE Output

Using the same time series of environmental data from each county‐based observing station, as well as the same priors established in Table 1, both the original (Nakase et al., 2023) and custom (this paper) MVSE packages were used to generate daily Index P. The daily Index P values were aggregated to monthly and annual means per location to fit the available case data from ArboNet. In the case of monthly Index P values, we use a 1‐month lag in our analysis because it better fits these data and is reasonable to assume human cases lag suitable environmental conditions due to both the delay in reporting cases (Parker, 2024) and transmission cycle (Moirano et al., 2025). Because we sampled environmental variables in counties with the highest incidence of WNND in each state, we assume they receive the plurality of cases in the state and are the best single location to represent environmental conditions for both the county and state. Though other papers calculate the Area Under the Curve for MVSE by summing daily values, we chose to take the mean (dividing by the number of days after summing) to account for 5 stations missing a small number of days.

For each location and time interval, Spearman's Rank Correlations (rS) and p‐values (α = 0.05) were calculated between the annual mean Index P for each package (original and custom) and the WNND and WNF incidence, with results summarized in Table 2, Tables 3, and 4. In general, an rS of 0.4 or higher is considered modestly‐well correlated, with values of 0.6 or higher considered strong and 0.8 or higher very strong. As for the correlation difference, any positive value suggests the custom model is an improvement over the original model in terms of Index P correlation with WNND or WNF. Small values indicate they are similarly correlated and negative values suggest the original is better correlated.

The Culex pipiens complex includes Cx. qinquefeasciatus and Cx. pipiens molestus that do not enter an overwintering dormancy or diapause, and Cx. pipiens pipiens that does (Aardema et al., 2022). This difference causes the habitat suitability for these species to vary by latitude (due to temperature differences), but the species can hybridize where their range overlaps. This likely makes our model better tuned to Cx. pipiens in northern latitudes, but we test it for a variety of geographies in the U.S. to determine if this is the case.

3 Data

Both annual, county‐level data and monthly, state‐level data for WNND and WNF cases were obtained from CDC's ArboNET for the years 2004–2019. These spatio‐temporal pairings are the two aggregation levels available from CDC, designed to protect patient confidentiality, so we work with both pairings rather than choosing one. This range was chosen to capture the interval of time after WNV had spread across the US but before the COVID‐19 pandemic to avoid the conditions that may have had an outsized impact on transmission such as lockdowns altering human behavior and exposure. West Nile Neuroinvasive Disease became a nationally notifiable disease in the United States in 2001, and WNF was added to the list in 2004 (Petersen, 2019), further justifying 2004 as a reasonable starting year for this analysis. WNF and WNND incidence rates were calculated annually using estimated county‐ or state‐level intercensal population estimates from the US Census Bureau. We use both disease severity types in our analysis to be comprehensive, though many authors choose to focus on WNND because its cases are more reliably reported. The top 20 counties chosen for our analysis (see Table 2) were the highest ranked according to WNND incidence, provided that they had a population of at least 500,000 people in any year during our period of interest and were not adjacent to each other. In the case of multiple adjacent counties with high rates, we selected the county nearest the climate observing station used.

Daily climate observing station data on air temperature at two m above the ground and RH were obtained using the R package GSODR (Sparks et al., 2017) to retrieve NOAA's Global Summary of the Day primarily from airport Automated Surface Observing System stations serving our counties of interest. In the case of multiple station options, the station within 40 km of the US Census Bureau's Center of Population for the county and with the fewest missing days was used. Five of the stations (Bakersfield, CA; Grand Rapids, MI; Modesto, CA; Phoenix, AZ; Tucson, AZ) were missing data on a small fraction (<0.2%) of days, but we consider this acceptable for in situ observations.

4 Results—Custom MVSE Outperforms Original at County‐Annual, But Not State‐Monthly Scale

4.1 General Differences in MVSE Outcomes When Employing Cx. Pipiens Parameters

To demonstrate the differences in the original and custom MVSE packages, a complete grid of all temperature (15–35C) and RH (0–100) combinations was fed to each package to produce heatmaps of key variables as shown in Figure 4. Relative humidity is shown rather than VPD because that is the input to both packages, and because the original package does not use VPD. It is important to caveat this approach to running MVSE by noting that the MCMC process fits the scaling coefficients using both the distributions of the mosquito behavior parameters and the available environmental data, so calibrating MVSE to a data set representing such extreme ranges of temperature and humidity will not perfectly exemplify its performance when fit with more appropriate observed environmental inputs.

Figure 4 Heatmaps of simulated mean Index P, lifespan, biting rate, and Extrinsic Incubation Period using the two Mosquito‐borne Viral Suitability Estimator packages: original (a) and custom (b). Each package was fed a filled grid of temperatures ranging from 15to35°C and relative humidities ranging from 1 to 100.

The original package in panel A shows a clear bias toward higher Index P values at high humidity while the custom package shows a more moderate bias toward higher Index P values at low humidity. This suggests that the switch from RH to VPD made the effect of humidity on transmission suitability less extreme. The difference in ideal temperatures for transmission was much less different between packages, with 28°C being optimum in each case.

The remaining plots in Figure 4 show how the joint effect of temperature and humidity affect mosquito lifespan, biting rate, and EIP. As expected, lifespan is longest at low temperatures in the custom package, whereas it is longest at high temperatures in the original package—traits better suited to Culex spp. in the custom package and Aedes spp. in the original package. The importance of biting rate increasing at higher VPD appears to have had a major effect on how humidity influences Index P. Biting rate is most frequent at high temperatures and low humidity in the custom package but is most frequent at high humidity and temperature agnostic in the original package. The general pattern of the effect of temperature on EIP is the same in both packages. However, the custom package displays much more stochasticity as a result of its values being sampled from the pre‐specified Weibull distribution (Figure 3) rather than deterministically calculated using a set formula as in the original package.

4.2 The Custom MSVE Package Outperforms the Original in Associations With WNND in a Majority of Counties

In the county‐annual analysis, the original package Index P was significantly correlated (95% CI, n = 16 years) with incidence of WNND in Los Angeles. The custom Index P package was significantly correlated with WNND incidence in Chicago (Cook County), Detroit (Wayne County), Hempstead (Nassau County), Omaha (Douglas County), and Los Angeles (Los Angeles County). It was an improvement over the original package in 15 of 20 locations (positive correlation differences indicate a stronger fit to the cases), though slightly worse in Los Angeles compared to the original model (Table 2). In the case of WNF incidence, the original package's Index P was not significantly correlated with the incidence of WNF in any location. The custom Index P was significantly correlated in Omaha (Douglas County), Chicago (Cook County), and Hempstead (Nassau County) and was an improvement over the original package in 16 of 20 locations (Table 3).

The stronger positive correlations of the custom package compared to the original package in most locations confirms that the modifications made to better simulate the temperature‐ and humidity‐sensitive traits of Cx. pipiens mosquitos were effective in improving the simulation of WNV transmission suitability. To understand why the improvements were not more consistent, we examined the correlation between the above correlations (between Index P and WNND/WNF incidence)—referred to as performance—and the habitat suitability for Culex pipiens. We also considered the maximum human population of any year for each location as a point of comparison since locations with a greater at‐risk population may have more robust surveillance data. We find that the performance of the package is moderately well correlated with Culex pipiens habitat suitability (WNND:rs=.48,p=.03;WNF:rs=.20,p=.41), and human population is slightly correlated with performance (WNND:rs=.37,p=.11;WNF:rs=.31,p=.18) as reported in Table 4.

We provide Index P correlations with both WNND and WNF incidence because either or both are used in much of the literature on WNV. While WNF cases are much more common than WNND cases, they are less likely to be detected due to milder symptoms, so WNND incidence is likely more accurately estimated (Holcomb et al., 2023). West Nile Neuroinvasive Disease represents a small but severe portion of the full burden of WNV, and it is less prone to zero‐inflation, so WNF is helpful to examine as well. As can be seen in Tables 2 and 3, there can be large differences in performance depending on which disease outcome we are considering. West Nile Neuroinvasive Disease is our preferred indicator of performance.

The improved performance in locations more likely to have Cx. pipiens present again underscores the importance of tuning models of transmission suitability to specific species. For example, while Cx. pipiens diapauses through the winter, Cx. quinquefasciatus does not (Meuti et al., 2015), so our assumption that lifespan increases linearly as temperatures cool does not apply to all Cx. spp. One caveat to our approach using modeled habitat suitability, however, is that this does not always correspond to observed mosquito distribution. For example, Los Angeles has a high Cx. pipiens habitat suitability (0.86) according to the study by Gorris et al. (2021), but field studies indicate that Cx. tarsalis and quinquefasciatus are the more dominant Cx. species (Barker et al., 2010). In the case of population versus performance, it may be that locations with larger populations will have more robust surveillance data (missing a few cases matters more if overall case counts are small).

Zooming in on one of the best‐fit locations for both WNND and WNF incidence, Chicago (Cook County), we see in Figure 5 (panel a) that the scaled time series of Index P from the custom package better fits the scaled WNND incidence than the original package. This mismatch is also clear when comparing the correlation of Index P from the two packages (panel b), which is very low (rs=.14,p=.59). In panel (c), we see that the variation of mean annual Index P values in the custom package (median = 0.46) is much lower than in the original package (median = 1.63).

At the monthly‐state scale all but one location performed well (rs> 0.4) in both MVSE packages: Phoenix (Maricopa County) (Table 5). The much stronger overall correlations between Index P and WNF or WNND incidence for most locations is likely the result of the strong seasonal variability in WNND and WNF cases, which is well captured in both the original and custom packages.

5 Discussion

The custom MVSE package performed better than the original package in correlating with WND cases in the case of the annual‐county scale model, especially in locations with a high habitat suitability for Culex pipiens mosquitos, demonstrating the importance of considering the species‐specific influence of environmental parameters on the behavior of mosquitos at these scales. The custom and original packages performed similarly in the monthly‐state scale model, suggesting that the customizations were less relevant to performance at those scales. Thus, the effect of the model changes was more apparent at a coarser temporal scale with a finer spatial scale. This is likely due to several factors. Many states are large enough to maintain a variety of climates, such as California which spans approximately 760 miles from north to south. Meanwhile Colorado varies in elevation by more than 11,000 feet. Given that the flight range of Culex mosquitos is generally less than one mile (Proença & Rebelo, 2024), refinements in spatial resolution for epidemiological, entomological, and environmental data would likely shrink observational error and improve correlations by more closely matching cases with their corresponding climate and mosquito species and behavior.

The performance of our custom model in California warrants specific consideration. We assessed model performance in Modesto, Fresno, Bakersfield, and Los Angeles, ordered here according to decreasing latitude. As Table 2 demonstrates, annual‐county model performance also declines with latitude in Modesto (0.31), Fresno (−0.15), and Bakersfield (−0.33) as would be expected with decreasing relative abundance of Cx. pipiens mosquitos. However, Los Angeles (0.55), the lowest latitude, performs well. It is possible that the tuning performed for Cx. pipiens made it a better fit for Cx. tarsalis which exhibit greater tolerance of low temperatures than Cx. quinquefasciatus (Moser et al., 2023), but this would be inconsistent with our explanation of poor model performance in Phoenix, which also contains populations of Culex tarsalis and quinquefasciatus. Phoenix does see much higher maximum temperatures than Los Angeles, which may contribute to the difference in performance. Other factors such as the ocean's influence on Los Angeles' climate versus the monsoon climate of Phoenix may also contribute to the differences. In both locations, Cx. tarsalis are present and do diapause, so future model studies that incorporate this mechanism would allow for further exploration of its importance in species‐specific tuning of Culex models.

We considered updating the package to include a diapause mechanism but did not for several reasons. The original MVSE package did not include this mechanism in the equation for Index P nor is it commonly included in formulations of R0. Index P is focused on the behavior of adult female mosquitos, but diapause‐induced developmental changes are initially triggered earlier in Culex pipiens development (Sanburg & Larsen, 1973). While photoperiod length is relatively easy to calculate given latitude, Armbruster (2016) reports that the Critical Photoperiod (when 50% of individuals enter diapause) for Culex pipiens can vary at the same latitude due to genetic differences. There are other complexities that would need to be better understood to properly include it in a model, including the interaction between photoperiod length and temperature (Field et al., 2022) and the influence of artificial light in urban environments (Fyie et al., 2021). Thus, while diapause is an important mechanism for understanding transmission of disease by Culex pipiens, more research under natural (not laboratory) conditions and an expanded mechanistic framework would be needed to properly accommodate this trait.

The poor performance of the custom model in arid regions of the U.S. overall could also be further investigated in future studies. While the replacement of RH with VPD presents a potential improvement to the mechanics of the model and its fit with biophysical reality (Brown et al., 2023), the assumption that mortality and biting rate are identically influenced by atmospheric moisture in scale, though opposite in direction, detracts from such an improvement. Further field studies exploring these traits, and the combined effect of temperature and VPD, are needed to test this assumption. Once complete, the custom model and the original MVSE framework it builds on, offer a starting point for subsequent model studies that can take advantage of future field work.

The similar model performance (original and custom) at the monthly scale could be due in part to the clear seasonal cycle in transmission that both models capture well month over month, which may be drowning out more subtle differences in model performance. Also, while we chose a 1‐month lag, it is possible that if we had weekly case data a lag of 3 or 4 weeks would have been more appropriate, in which case a 1‐month lag (effectively weeks 5–8) would not be the best choice for correlation. While we did not test weekly Index P against monthly case data, this would be a potential next step for diagnosing this issue.

Our choice of human cases as the outcome to test model predictions against also likely led to lower correlations. Index P, as a transmission suitability index, is capturing only one segment of the overall process of vectoring a virus. This transmission probability model does not incorporate mosquito breeding or density parameters, human behaviors such as engaging in mosquito control or creating refugia, population immunity, avian migration and distribution, or factors that may have influenced detection of cases such as population health and demographics that may influence the likelihood of a case becoming neuroinvasive (Jean et al., 2007).

The MVSE package is subject to several more limitations and assumptions. The distributions specified for species‐specific behavior (e.g., biting rate) upfront in the model are understood to represent the observations of traits under all environmental conditions throughout the year, but as the annual distribution of conditions (e.g., temperature) differs by climatic zone, the transferability of the distributions across large distances is questionable. The model also does not explicitly capture any autoregressive characteristics, such as behaviors that are sensitive to trait values in previous time steps. The current modeling framework, being tailored to one mosquito species, could be further enhanced by including a wider range of parameterizations for mosquito species known to transmit the focal disease (WND in this case). Finally, as is often noted in the literature on models of WNV, further disambiguation of the role of the avian host on transmission and incorporation into mechanistic models may lead to prediction skill gains.

The accessibility of an R package like MVSE makes Index P an easy component to incorporate in a variety of future studies, but the lack of a directly observable outcome for transmission makes it difficult to assess its skill. While we did not directly compare the mechanistic MVSE model to statistical models of the influence of environmental variables on disease outcomes in this study, we believe these results suggest Index P could be a useful component of such models. In locations where this custom MVSE model appears to perform well (e.g., Chicago, Omaha), comparing it to more spatiotemporally refined WNV case data sets such as weekly‐county scale data could be a next step.

6 Conclusions

By systematically adapting a mechanistic framework to the environmental sensitivity of Culex pipiens and integrating the robust metric of VPD, this study demonstrates that greater biological realism can yield a significantly improved model of WNV transmission suitability. Our findings show that these species‐specific customizations are supportive of explaining interannual variability in disease patterns at the county scale, and that integrating consideration of the sensitivity of life history traits to environmental factors into models can directly enhance tools for public health surveillance. This work also suggests needed research to improve the next generation of WNV models, which should now move beyond environmental drivers to incorporate the dynamic complexities of the vector community and critical life‐history strategies like photoperiod‐driven diapause. Achieving this will be an important next step in transitioning from retrospective explanation to the ultimate goal of skillful, preventative forecasting of WNV risk.
