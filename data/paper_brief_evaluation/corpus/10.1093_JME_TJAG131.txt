Abstract

Mosquitoes are important vectors of pathogens, including West Nile virus, in California’s Central Valley. Public interest in plant-based mosquito repellents is widespread. Local residents frequently inquire whether ornamental plants provide mosquito protection however, the effectiveness of plants as spatial repellents is uncertain. To address this, we screened 6 candidate ornamental plants using laboratory Y-tube olfactometer bioassays, plants screened included: white yarrow (Achillea millefolium, Linnaeus), lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus Stapf), lavender (Lavandula angustifolia, Miller), prostrate rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus ‘Prostratus’, Spenner), white sage (Salvia apiana, Jepson), and Citronella—Scented (CS) geranium (Pelargonium citrosum, L’Héritier). Citronella—Scented geranium and prostrate rosemary ranked highest in qualitative repellency scores and were selected for further examination based on retail availability. These 2 species were then evaluated in replicated field trials in both urban and rural locations over 16 wk. Treatments were rotated among sites using a Latin square design and compared against standard surveillance controls and a metofluthrin-based spatial repellent (Thermacell E90). Neither plant treatment reduced catch rates of host-seeking mosquitoes relative to controls under passive or mechanically damaged conditions at either location, while the metofluthrin treatment produced consistently lower mosquito captures at both locations. These results suggest that whole ornamental plants do not provide spatial repellency under realistic field deployment conditions.

Introduction

Mosquitoes are among the most important arthropods affecting human health worldwide, serving as vectors of pathogens such as West Nile, dengue, and Zika viruses. Their nuisance value drives public demand for mosquito control interventions (Halasa et al. 2014, Lee et al. 2018, Prudêncio 2020). In northern California, Culex tarsalis (Coquillett) and Culex pipiens (Linnaeus) are primary vectors of West Nile virus, while the invasive Aedes aegypti (Linnaeus) continues to expand its geographic range, raising concern about future arbovirus transmission (CDPH 2016, 2024, McGregor and Connelly 2021). Both disease prevention and nuisance reduction are the primary goals of mosquito management programs.

During community outreach and public education events, the Sacramento—Yolo Mosquito and Vector Control District (SYMVCD) staff are regularly questioned about whether ornamental plants offer meaningful mosquito protection, reflecting broader public interest in natural alternatives to conventional control methods (Maia and Moore 2011, Moore et al. 2018). This interest centers on the idea that planting certain ornamental species in home gardens may passively deter mosquitoes without the use of chemical products. Numerous plant-derived essential oils contain volatile compounds capable of repelling mosquitoes when extracted, concentrated, and formulated into topical or vapor-dispersing products (Barnard 1999, Sritabutra and Soonwera 2013, Luker et al. 2023). However, the mechanisms underlying these effects differ substantially from those expected from intact living plants. Extracted oils and commercial formulations deliver active ingredients at concentrations specifically engineered to volatilize and disperse within treated spaces, whereas living plants often emit volatile compounds passively and at comparatively low rates (Effah et al. 2022, Midzi et al. 2022, Widhalm et al. 2023). In comparison, synthetic spatial repellents, such as metofluthrin, are specifically formulated to volatilize at concentrations sufficient to modify mosquito behavior (Couvillon et al. 2023). Environmental factors such as airflow, dilution, plant morphology, and distance from emission sources may further limit the formation of odor concentrations necessary to influence mosquito behavior. Physical disturbance of plant tissue, such as that caused by herbivory or handling, is known to increase volatile emission rates (Wardle et al. 2003) and could in principle increase concentrations closer to repellency thresholds. Despite widespread promotion of ornamental plants as natural mosquito deterrents, controlled field evaluations of whole, intact living plants remain lacking in the published literature.

Two ornamental plants frequently marketed for mosquito repellency are Citronella—scented (CS) geranium (Pelargonium citrosum) and prostrate rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus ‘Prostratus’). Citronella—scented geranium produces volatile compounds including citronellol and geraniol, which have demonstrated repellent activity in extracted and concentrated forms (Kongkaew et al. 2011, Maia and Moore 2011, Mitra et al. 2020, Houzi et al. 2024). Similarly, prostrate rosemary emits monoterpenes such as camphor and 1,8-cineole, which have shown insecticidal and repellent properties at high concentrations in laboratory settings (Klocke et al. 1987, Houzi et al. 2024, Lopez et al. 2025). Whether ambient emissions from intact living specimens of either species are sufficient to deter host-seeking mosquitoes under field conditions has not been rigorously established.

To address this, the SYMVCD conducted a controlled study to assess the spatial repellent efficacy of ornamental plants under urban and rural conditions. Candidate plants were first selected based on ranked repellency scores across initial Y-tube bioassay trials of white yarrow (Achillea millefolium), lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus), white sage (Salvia apiana), lavender (Lavandula angustifolia), prostrate rosemary and CS geranium. Citronella—Scented geranium and prostrate rosemary ranked highest in qualitative repellency among the candidate plants and were further selected based on their widespread retail availability. We then evaluated these plants against no repellent control and a metofluthrin-based spatial repellent using replicated trap deployments over 16 wk, with both passive and mechanically damaged plant conditions, in urban and rural locations.

Materials and Methods

Laboratory Assay

Repellency bioassays were conducted by Carroll-Loye Biological Research (Davis, California) using a standard World Health Organization (WHO) Y-tube olfactometer to screen 6 candidate plant treatments under binary choice conditions prior to field deployment. Briefly, treatments included white yarrow, lemongrass, lavender, white sage, prostrate rosemary, and CS geranium. Sunflower (Helianthus annuus, Linnaeus) was included as non-repellent ornamental reference. All plants were purchased from Green Acres Nursery and Supply (Elk Grove, California, United States) in standard 3.7 L nursery containers. Plants were selected based on uniform size and health, with heights ranging from 15 to 25 cm. All specimens were in vegetative growth stage without active flowering at the time of purchase. Plants were watered daily and maintained outdoors under ambient conditions for 5 d prior to bioassay testing to allow for acclimation and to ensure consistent physiological condition across all treatments.

The Y-tube olfactometer had an internal diameter of approximately 9 cm, with each upper arm approximately 20 cm long and a main stem approximately 45 cm long. Each upper arm was connected to a plexiglass enclosure via approximately 45 cm of tapered aluminum tubing that narrowed from 15.6 cm at the enclosure intake to match the 9 cm internal diameter of the Y-tube. Each test plant was housed individually within one of these plexiglass enclosures, which had a circular intake port approximately 15.6 cm in diameter (Fig. 1). Each test plant was tested in its pot with soil; the opposing enclosure was left empty and served as the clean-air control. Plant treatments were alternated between the arms of the Y-tube across replicates to control for any directional bias. The intake of each enclosure was oriented away from the opposing enclosure, so that air was drawn through the enclosure, across the plant, through the tapered tubing, and into the Y-tube arm. From the base of the Y-tube, approximately 1.8 m of aluminum tubing connected to the intake of a fixed wind tunnel (46 cm × 46 cm × 122 cm), with an exhaust fan positioned downstream and connected to the wind tunnel by an additional approximately 1.8 m of tubing. This arrangement produced unidirectional airflow originating at each plexiglass enclosure, traveling through the tapered tubing, down the arms, through the main stem, along the aluminum tubing, through the wind tunnel, and out via the exhaust fan. Air speed was measured within the mosquito holding chamber at the base of the Y-tube, downstream of the fork, and fan speed was adjusted to achieve the target velocity at that location.

For each replicate a group of mixed-age, laboratory-reared Ae. aegypti females were aspirated from the main colony cage into the holding chamber which was then inserted into the base of the Y-tube and confined for a 2-min acclimation period with no airflow through the olfactometer. Airflow and CO2 delivery were then initiated, and the mosquitoes were released into the main stem, after which they were given 5 to 8 min to make a directional choice. CO2 was delivered at 0.15 L/min into both arms equally to standardize host-odor background and isolate the effect of plant volatiles on mosquito orientation. All assays were conducted during daytime hours inside a climate-controlled laboratory under consistent temperature, humidity, and lighting conditions. The person conducting the assay set up and ran each trial, following the same procedure throughout. Between trials, the entire olfactometer system was cleaned with ethanol and flushed with clean air for 10 min to remove residual odors before the next assay.

Each plant species was represented by a single individual and was tested across multiple replicates (range: 5 to 7 replicates per species). Each replicate consisted of exposing a fresh group of 7 to 18 female mosquitoes, based on colony availability, to the same plant individual. Females were not reused between replicates. Repellency was calculated for each replicate as the proportion of the total mosquitoes released, including individuals that made no directional choice, that oriented toward the clean air arm, consistent with WHO olfactometer guidelines (WHO 2013). Treating undecided individuals as non-responders retained in the denominator provides a conservative estimate of repellency under conditions of variable mosquito responsiveness. Plants were ranked by estimated probability of clean air orientation, and the 2 highest-ranking species were selected for field evaluation based on this ranking and their commercial availability.

Field Trial

Climate

Sacramento has a Mediterranean climate (Köppen Csa) characterized by hot, dry summers and mild, wetter winters. The study period from early July through late October spanned the warmest and driest part of the year. Average daily high temperatures declined from approximately 35 °C in July to the mid-20s°C by late October, with overnight lows ranging from about 15 °C to 10 °C, and rainfall was minimal throughout, with essentially dry conditions in July and August and only slight increases in September and October.

Rural Location

Four trapping sites were established on a single property in Elk Grove, California, United States, which granted access for the duration of the study. The surrounding landscape of irrigated pastures and agricultural land supports high Culex and Aedes spp. abundance. Sites were distributed across an open field with a minimum inter trap spacing of 75 m to minimize cross-treatment interference (Marinković et al. 2014)

Urban Location

Four sites were established in the Upper Land Park neighborhood of Sacramento, California. Residential properties were selected in areas of known Ae. aegypti infestation with and average in between residence distance of 180 m. The local residents granted property access for trap deployment, and no personal data was collected. Urban field sites were specifically selected to target Aedes aegypti, the species utilized in the Y-tube bioassay, and a growing nuisance in the SYMVCD service area that heavily utilizes urban landscapes. The neighborhood is an established residential area of single-family homes with landscaped yards and ornamental plantings set within a mature urban tree canopy that includes London plane, valley oak, interior live oak, blue oak, and redwood. This irrigated, vegetated residential setting provides shaded harborage and abundant container habitat for Ae. aegypti.

Mosquito Collection

All mosquitoes were collected using BG-Sentinel traps (BG-S; Biogents AG, Regensburg, Germany), urban sites were supplemented with BG-Lure (active ingredients: lactic acid 34.59%, ammonium bicarbonate 26.49%, hexanoic acid 8.82%; Biogents AG Regensburg, Germany) in addition to CO2 to maximize Ae. aegypti collections. In addition, all traps were set with CO2 delivered at 0.35 L/min (calibrated by flow meter) to simulate human respiratory output (Cai et al. 2022). Full liquid CO2 tanks (9 kg) were deployed at each site at the start of each week and remained in place for the duration of the weekly deployment, providing sufficient capacity for continuous emission without interruption. All BG-S were powered with 12 V, 14.0 amp hour sealed lead-acid batteries that were exchanged and recharged after each 24 h trap period. Each deployment consisted of a single continuous 24 h trap period (traps set at approximately 0900 h and serviced at approximately 0900 h the following day). The trial was conducted over 16 consecutive weeks from early July through late October, yielding 439 trap total trap periods.

Treatments

Citronella—Scented geranium and prostrate rosemary were selected for field deployment. Plants were sourced and selected as described above. Plant treatments consisted of 9 potted, species-matched specimens, that formed a continuous barrier around the base of the BG-S. The entry port is located at the top of the trap and remained unobstructed by the plant canopy, so that treatment effects were attributable to plant volatile emissions rather than physical obstruction of the trap. Separate plant groups were maintained for each location (urban and rural) and rotated among sites with each treatment. Replacement plants were maintained under identical outdoor conditions throughout the study but were not needed. Plants were potted in 30 cm (18 L) containers using GreenAll Natural and Organic Potting Soil (E.B. Stone, San Jose, California) amended with Osmocote slow-release fertilizer (ICL Growing Solutions, Geldermalsen, Netherlands) at the manufacturer’s recommended rate and watered and monitored daily for flowering state and overall health throughout the study. No plants flowered during the study period and maintained consistent vegetative growth across all treatments.

A chemical spatial repellent treatment consisted of a Thermacell E90 Rechargeable Mosquito Repeller (Thermacell Repellents, Inc., Bedford, Massachusetts), which volatilizes metofluthrin (5.5% active ingredient). The device was equipped with 40-hour refill cartridges and powered by a 10,000 mAh portable battery pack, providing sufficient capacity for uninterrupted 24 h operation as verified during laboratory testing prior to field use. The Thermacell was positioned immediately adjacent to the BG-S and served as a direct comparison to plant treatments. A BG-S set without plants or Thermacell served as untreated, or no repellent control, using host-seeking mosquito catch rates as a proxy for the repellency effect. Thus, there were 4 different treatments deployed at both rural and urban study locations: (i) CS geranium with CO2, (ii) prostrate rosemary with CO2, (iii) Thermacell E90 with CO2, and (iv) and CO2 only.

Experimental Design

Treatments were assigned to sites using a 4 × 4 Latin square implemented on a weekly basis. Each weekly replicate consisted of 4 consecutive 24 h trap periods, with each treatment deployed once at each of the 4 sites, completing one full Latin square rotation per week. Each mosquito collection represented a single continuous 24 h trap period from a unique site and treatment combination. The study was conducted for 16 wk from early July through late October, with a total of 439 trap periods. Traps were maintained daily, at each visit the trap bag from the previous 24 h period collected and returned to the lab, plants were rotated as prescribed by the study plan, and a new catch bag was placed. At the SYMVCD laboratory bags were placed in a −80 °C freezer to kill mosquitoes prior to identification and enumeration.

Study Phases

The study was conducted in 2 phases to evaluate the effect of mechanical tissue damage on plant repellency. During Phase 1 (weeks 1 to 8), plants were maintained without manipulation, testing whether potential passive ambient volatile emissions alone were sufficient to deter host-seeking mosquitoes. During Phase 2 (weeks 9 to 16), a trimming protocol was introduced as the only modification to the standard deployment: plants were trimmed immediately upon deployment at each new site, removing 5 g of foliage per plant per trimming event (∼10 g per plant per week), with clippings cut into approximately 1 cm pieces and redistributed within the pot to simulate incidental mechanical disturbance consistent with routine garden maintenance and to maximize potential volatile release during each 24 h collection period. All other aspects of the rotation, trap configuration, and collection protocol remained identical across both phases.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analysis was conducted in R version 4.3.3 (R Core Team 2024). Laboratory bioassay data were analyzed using a grouped binomial generalized linear model (GLM) with a logit link function, where successes represented mosquitoes orienting to the clean air arm and failures represented all remaining individuals, including those choosing the plant arm and those making no directional choice, retaining undecided mosquitoes in the denominator. Sunflower was included as a non-repellent ornamental reference. An overall treatment effect was evaluated using a likelihood ratio test comparing the full model to an intercept-only null model. Estimated marginal means and pairwise contrasts between each treatment and the Sunflower non-repellent ornamental reference were computed on the response scale with 95% confidence intervals using the emmeans package (Lenth 2024), with P-values adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. To assess whether any treatment produced a non-random orientation response, each treatment was additionally tested against a null probability of 0.50 using exact binomial tests, with Bonferroni correction applied across all 7 comparisons.

Mosquito trap counts were modeled using negative binomial generalized linear models to account for overdispersion. Treatment was included as a fixed effect with the untreated BG-S trap (no repellent control) as the reference level. Site and week were included as fixed blocking factors to account for the Latin square experimental design. Models were fitted using the glm.nb function from the MASS package (Venables and Ripley 2002). An overall treatment effect was evaluated for each model using a likelihood ratio test comparing the full model to a reduced model excluding treatment. Estimated marginal means and pairwise contrasts between each treatment and the no repellent control were computed on the response scale as incidence rate ratios (IRR) with 95% confidence intervals using the emmeans package (Lenth 2024). Percent reductions were calculated as (1 − IRR) × 100. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction applied across 3 contrasts within each model. Statistical significance was assessed at α = 0.05. Subsequent analysis focused on Cx. pipiens, Cx. tarsalis, Ae. melanimon (Dyar), and Ae. vexans (Meigen) at rural locations and Ae. aegypti and Culiseta incidens (Thomson) at urban locations, selected based on abundance and epidemiological relevance, well as total female abundance to capture overall community level responses. Males were excluded from all analyses as only females engage in host-seeking behavior and blood feeding. Complete statistical output for all models, including estimated marginal means, contrasts, and adjusted P values, is provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S4.

Results

Laboratory Assay

A grouped binomial GLM detected no overall treatment effect on mosquito orientation (LRT: χ2 = 10.47, df = 6, P = 0.106), and no treatment differed significantly from the Sunflower non-repellent ornamental reference after Bonferroni correction (all P ≥ 0.65). Clean air orientation (the proportion of all released mosquitoes orienting to the clean air arm), with each treatment tested against random choice by exact binomial test (Bonferroni-corrected), was: Sunflower 40.0% (95% CI: 30.2 to 50.7%; n = 85, 6 replicates; P = 0.574), CS geranium 52.9% (41.2 to 64.2%; n = 70, 6 replicates; P = 1.000), lavender 38.3% (28.4 to 49.3%; n = 81, 6 replicates; P = 0.314), lemongrass 30.6% (21.0 to 42.1%; n = 72, 6 replicates; P = 0.009), prostrate rosemary 50.0% (37.8 to 62.2%; n = 62, 5 replicates; P = 1.000), white sage 47.2% (36.0 to 58.7%; n = 72, 6 replicates; P = 1.000), and white yarrow 40.0% (29.9 to 51.0%; n = 80, 7 replicates; P = 0.650). Only lemongrass differed significantly from random orientation, with mosquitoes orienting toward the plant arm more often than the clean air arm, indicating attraction rather than repellency. No other treatment differed from random choice. Although no treatment differed significantly from random orientation, CS geranium and prostrate rosemary ranked highest among the 6 candidate species and were selected for field evaluation on this basis in combination with their widespread retail availability.

Field Trial

A total of 44,527 mosquitoes from 9 species were collected across rural and urban locations throughout the study period (Table 1). Of the total, 43,846 (98.5%) were female, with males accounting for 681 individuals (1.5%). Rural location contributed 91.1% of the total catch while urban location accounted for the remaining 8.9%. Across the rural sites, the most abundant species were Cx. pipiens (41.8%), Cx. tarsalis (25.6%) Aedes melanimon (Dyar) (26.3%), and Aedes vexans (Meigen) (5.4%). For the urban sites, Ae. aegypti represented 52.5% and Cs. Incidens (Thomson) was 16% of the total catch.

Rural Location Sites

During Phase 1 (passive emissions), neither CS geranium nor prostrate rosemary differed significantly from the no repellent control for any species after Bonferroni correction (all P = 1.000). For Cx. pipiens (LRT: χ2 = 56.25, df = 3, P < 0.001), mean catches per 24 h trap periodwere 125.4 (±97.4 SD) for no repellent control, 120.5 (±101.1) for CS geranium (IRR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.52 to 1.76, P = 1.000), and 169.9 (±190.1) for prostrate rosemary (IRR = 1.21, 95% CI: 0.65 to 2.25, P = 1.000). For Cx. tarsalis (LRT: χ2 = 21.80, df = 3, P < 0.001), mean catches were 71.6 (±44.5) for no repellent control, 57.4 (±40.1) for CS geranium (IRR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.49 to 1.25, P = 0.651), and 70.5 (±52.4) for prostrate rosemary (IRR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.57 to 1.46, P = 1.000). For total females (LRT: χ2 = 44.44, df = 3, P < 0.001), mean catches were 312.1 (±217.5) for no repellent control, 299.3 (±226.1) for CS geranium (IRR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.59 to 1.52, P = 1.000), and 354.9 (±295.3) for prostrate rosemary (IRR = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.66 to 1.71, P = 1.000). Thermacell produced significant reductions relative to the no repellent control across all species: 89.5% for Cx. pipiens (IRR = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.20, P < 0.001), 61.8% for Cx. tarsalis (IRR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.24 to 0.61, P < 0.001), 70.1% for Ae. melanimon (IRR = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.19 to 0.47, P < 0.001), 83.7% for Ae. vexans (IRR = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.09 to 0.31, P < 0.001), and 74.7% for total females (IRR = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.16 to 0.41, P < 0.001).

During Phase 2 (mechanical damage), plant treatments again showed no significant reductions relative to the no repellent control for any species. For Cx. pipiens (LRT: χ2 = 74.28, df = 3, P < 0.001), mean catches were 51.5 (±39.0) for no repellent control, 67.9 (±44.2) for CS geranium (IRR = 1.24, 95% CI: 0.79 to 1.95, P = 0.754), and 73.2 (±55.8) for prostrate rosemary (IRR = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.78 to 1.92, P = 0.883). For Cx. tarsalis (LRT: χ2 = 43.03, df = 3, P < 0.001), prostrate rosemary catches were significantly higher than the no repellent control (IRR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.08 to 2.12, P = 0.010), as were CS geranium catches for Ae. vexans (LRT: χ2 = 25.78, df = 3, P < 0.001; IRR = 2.53, 95% CI: 1.01 to 6.30, P = 0.046), indicating attraction rather than repellency under mechanically damaged conditions. No other plant treatment contrasts were significant for Ae. melanimon (LRT: χ2 = 42.29, df = 3, P < 0.001). For total females (LRT: χ2 = 72.13, df = 3, P < 0.001), mean catches were 111.3 (±68.4) for no repellent control, 163.2 (±100.1) for CS geranium (IRR = 1.33, 95% CI: 0.93 to 1.91, P = 0.181), and 163.7 (±99.1) for prostrate rosemary (IRR = 1.28, 95% CI: 0.89 to 1.84, P = 0.325). Thermacell continued to produce significant reductions compared to the no repellent control across all species: 82.9% for Cx. pipiens (IRR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.11 to 0.28, P < 0.001), 43.2% for Cx. tarsalis (IRR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.40 to 0.81, P = 0.0003), 67.1% for Ae. melanimon (IRR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.20 to 0.54, P < 0.001), 80.6% for Ae. vexans (IRR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.07 to 0.58, P = 0.001), and 69.3% for total females (IRR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.21 to 0.44, P < 0.001).

Urban Location Sites

During Phase 1, mean Ae. aegypti catches per 24 h trap periodwere 3.96 (±3.10) for no repellent control, 4.39 (±3.79) for CS geranium (IRR = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.73 to 1.72, P = 1.000), and 4.57 (±2.46) for prostrate rosemary (IRR = 1.16, 95% CI: 0.76 to 1.78, P = 1.000) (LRT: χ2 = 17.77, df = 3, P < 0.001). For Cs. incidens (LRT: χ2 = 14.58, df = 3, P = 0.002), mean catches were 6.22 (±9.15) for no repellent control, 8.30 (±10.47) for CS geranium (IRR = 1.44, 95% CI: 0.76 to 2.75, P = 0.530), and 6.17 (±6.98) for prostrate rosemary (IRR = 1.16, 95% CI: 0.61 to 2.24, P = 1.000). Neither plant treatment differed significantly from the no repellent control for either species after Bonferroni correction. Thermacell produced a 49.2% reduction in Ae. aegypti (IRR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.31 to 0.85, P = 0.005) and a 56.3% reduction in Cs. incidens (IRR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.21 to 0.90, P = 0.018) relative to the no repellent control.

During Phase 2, mean catches increased across all treatments. For Ae. aegypti (LRT: χ2 = 77.89, df = 3, P < 0.001), mean catches were 10.97 (±6.78) for no repellent control, 13.39 (±8.95) for CS geranium (IRR = 1.18, 95% CI: 0.87 to 1.60, P = 0.592), and 10.00 (±6.42) for prostrate rosemary (IRR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.67 to 1.26, P = 1.000). Neither plant treatment showed evidence of repellent activity under mechanically damaged conditions. Thermacell again had significantly fewer Ae. aegypti females than the no repellent control, representing a 74.2% reduction (IRR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.18 to 0.38, P < 0.001). Culiseta incidens was not modeled separately in Phase 2 because catches were too sparse to support a reliable model.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to provide a controlled laboratory and realistic field evaluation of whole ornamental plants commonly marketed for mosquito repellency. Plants were tested in a practical context like those experienced by local residents. The plants selected for this study were readily commercially available, potted in standard containers, and routinely maintained. Assessing the repellent potential of these widely marketed species has direct public health relevance, as local residents commonly purchase and deploy them for personal mosquito protection.

Mosquito repellency encompasses several behavioral mechanisms: contact repellents function only after landing, whereas spatial repellents act through airborne chemicals that modify mosquito movement before host contact occurs (Dickens and Bohbot 2013, Afify and Potter 2020, Iovinella et al. 2022, Portilla Pulido et al. 2022). Additional behavioral effects, including deterrence and attraction inhibition, may reduce mosquito activity without producing true repellency. Host-seeking mosquitoes depend primarily on olfactory cues to locate vertebrate hosts (Zwiebel and Takken 2004, Qiu and van Loon 2010, Dekker and Cardé 2011), with CO2 acting as a long-range behavioral activator that sensitizes mosquitoes to secondary host cues such as skin odors, heat, and moisture (Khan et al. 1966, McMeniman et al. 2014, Majeed et al. 2017). Host detection occurs across odor gradients in which attraction and avoidance responses are mediated by concentration and environmental dispersion (Wolff and Riffell 2018, Morita et al. 2025). Effective repellency therefore requires disruption of host detection at distances relevant to mosquito flight behavior.

The selection of CS geranium and prostrate rosemary for field evaluation was based on their widespread retail availability as well as their ranking among 6 candidate species in the laboratory bioassay, with orientation proportions of 52.9% and 50.0%, respectively. This ranking-based selection is acknowledged as qualitative; the orientation proportions of species were statistically indistinguishable from random choice, and the ranking itself may reflect stochastic variation in mosquito behavior rather than true differences in repellent potential among species. The laboratory and field results consistently failed to detect repellent activity from these ornamental species, and the field evaluation confirmed that none of the tested species showed statistically significant repellency in the laboratory, under realistic deployment conditions. While CS geranium and prostrate rosemary are known to produce compounds with repellent activity in extracted and concentrated forms (Klocke et al. 1987, Kongkaew et al. 2011, Maia and Moore 2011, Houzi et al. 2024, Lopez et al. 2025), passive emissions from living specimens appear insufficient to create the odor gradients necessary to modify mosquito flight behavior at ecologically relevant distances. Regardless of whether plants were maintained passively or subjected to mechanical damage, neither species reduced host seeking mosquito catch rates relative to controls under field conditions. In contrast, the metofluthrin-based spatial repellent consistently reduced mosquito catches by 43 to 89% across all deployment scenarios, demonstrating that the experimental design could detect repellent effects when present.

The introduction of mechanical tissue damage during Phase 2 was designed to test whether physical disturbance might enhance repellent activity. Tissue damage can trigger enhanced emission of plant volatiles as part of herbivore defense responses (Piesik et al. 2006, Smith and Beck 2013, Yuan et al. 2025), however, we did not directly measure volatile emissions to confirm whether our mechanical damage protocol increased volatile release from the test species. The lack of enhanced repellent activity could reflect several possibilities: insufficient volatile increase from the damage protocol, volatile levels that remained below behavioral thresholds even if increased, or induction of compounds different from those responsible for repellent activity. However, even with regular trimming, neither species showed evidence of repellent activity. The trimming protocol simulated routine garden maintenance while maximizing potential volatile release; more intensive damage would likely compromise plant health and fall outside realistic local residents’ practices.

Results were consistent across mosquito genera and habitat types, with both Culex species in rural agricultural settings and Aedes aegypti in urban residential areas showing no response to plant treatments. This cross-species consistency demonstrates that the lack of repellent effect is not species-specific or limited to environmental conditions. The higher overall mosquito abundance during Phase 2, particularly notable in the urban location where Ae. aegypti catches nearly tripled, reflected seasonal population dynamics as the study progressed from mid-summer through fall (Barker et al. 2010, Thiemann et al. 2012, Sehi et al. 2025). While increased mosquito pressure might theoretically make repellent effects more readily detectable, high population densities can also overwhelm repellent mechanisms through chance encounters (Barnard et al. 1998), similar to observations with mating disruption pheromones at high pest densities (Daane et al. 2021, Adamič Zamljen et al. 2025). Regardless of the underlying mechanism, plant treatments remained ineffective across both low and high abundance periods. Conversely, the consistent efficacy of the metofluthrin treatment validates the experimental approach and demonstrates that repellent effects can be detected under our study conditions. Metofluthrin is specifically formulated to volatilize at concentrations engineered to disrupt mosquito behavior, representing a fundamentally different approach than relying on passive plant emissions (Couvillon et al. 2023, Kim et al. 2023, Agumba et al. 2024). The device used in this study delivers active ingredients at controlled rates designed to maintain effective concentration within its operational radius.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. Volatile emissions from both plant species were not directly quantified during field deployment. The observed lack of repellent activity is therefore consistent with but does not mechanistically confirm emission rates insufficient to reach behavioral thresholds under ambient field conditions. The study was conducted during a single season and may not capture potential variations in plant volatile production related to phenological stage, environmental stress, or plant maturity. Additionally, the specimens used were relatively young nursery plants; larger, more mature individuals with greater foliar biomass may emit higher volatile quantities and could perform differently than observed here. The specific cultivars tested represent only a subset of available plant varieties, and results may not generalize all genotypes within these species. Environmental conditions, including temperature, humidity, and wind patterns, were not controlled and may have influenced both volatile dispersion and mosquito behavior independently of treatment effects. Additionally, the study design focused on 24 h trap periods, and spatial replication was limited to representative rural and urban locations rather than multiple locations within each habitat type.

These findings have direct implications for public education and local resident expectations. The widespread marketing of ornamental plants as mosquito deterrents appears to be based primarily on the repellent properties of concentrated plant extracts rather than empirical evaluation of intact living specimens under realistic conditions (Maia and Moore 2011, Moore et al. 2018). By trying to mimic residential deployment, using nursery-purchased plants in standard containers positioned as they might be arranged around patios or outdoor seating areas, this study provides local residents and vector control agencies with directly applicable evidence. While these plant species may offer aesthetic and culinary value, or even repellency properties against different arthropod pests, passive placement of intact living plants does not provide the spatial mosquito protection that retail marketing implies. Vector control agencies should address this misconception during public outreach while promoting evidence-based alternatives including source reduction, personal protection, and scientifically validated treatments.

Future research could explore quantitative measurement of volatile emissions and their spatial dispersion to provide mechanistic insight into concentration gradients achieved. Testing alternative plant species selected specifically for high volatile production might identify candidates with greater field efficacy. Evaluation of enclosed or semi-enclosed spaces such as covered patios, screened porches, or greenhouse environments could reveal conditions where volatile accumulation enhances plant repellency effectiveness. Finally, investigation of plant-based repellency combined with other integrated pest management approaches might identify synergistic effects not apparent from individual treatments.
