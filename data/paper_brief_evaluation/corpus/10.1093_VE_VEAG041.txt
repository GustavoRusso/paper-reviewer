Abstract

Mosquito-borne viruses are expanding their geographic range in Europe, increasingly affecting wildlife and human health. Usutu virus (USUV), a zoonotic mosquito-borne Orthoflavivirus with birds as amplifying hosts, has caused large-scale avian mortality in Europe, particularly among Eurasian Blackbirds (Turdus merula). Although USUV is not considered a major human health threat, it is closely related to other human-pathogenic arboviruses. Its emergence in northwestern Europe may indicate increasingly suitable conditions for other arboviruses that could follow similar transmission dynamics upon emergence in this setting. In the Netherlands, USUV was first detected in 2016, caused major outbreaks in birds until 2018, and resurged in 2022. Here, we conduct phylodynamic and phylogeographic analyses to reconstruct the emergence and persistence dynamics of USUV in the Netherlands. We analyse 298 near-full-length viral genomes obtained from birds and mosquitoes from the Netherlands, combined with publicly available USUV genomes. We identify multiple independent USUV introductions into the Netherlands region, leading to the apparent enzootic establishment of two predominant clades from lineage Africa 3 in this broader region. For these two clades, our analyses show a temporal pattern consistent with invasion followed by local persistence, associated with a substantial spatial intermixing of viral lineages circulating in the region. Landscape phylogeographic analyses further suggest a possible tendency for USUV lineages to circulate in urbanized areas and regions with higher human population density, though future work should assess whether this pattern reflects true ecological preference or reporting bias. In a context with recurrent outbreaks of West Nile virus in northwestern Europe, this study provides insight into the dynamics of emergence and establishment of a closely related mosquito-borne virus in a previously nonendemic temperate region.

Introduction

Mosquito-borne viruses pose a major threat to the health of human and animal populations, with substantial economic consequences. Since the early 2000s, Europe has experienced an increasing number of mosquito-borne disease outbreaks and an expansion of the geographical range of arboviruses on the continent (Barzon 2018, European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) 2025). This trend is partly driven by climate change (Semenza and Suk 2018, Koch et al. 2024), which, through rising temperatures, favours two overlapping processes: (i) the establishment of invasive Aedes mosquito species, enabling local transmission of viruses such as dengue and chikungunya viruses following introductions through travellers, and (ii) longer Culex pipiens activity seasons, higher seasonal abundance, and enhanced replication of viruses such as West Nile and Usutu viruses in these locally common vectors. In addition to rising temperatures, factors including land-use and habitat changes, travel and trade, and fluctuations in water accessibility and quality can also impact arbovirus transmission dynamics (Norris 2004, Tatem et al. 2006, Avramov et al. 2024). The cumulative effects of these environmental changes on arboviruses, vectors, and hosts are expected to intensify, placing arboviruses among priority climate-sensitive diseases (Caminade et al. 2018, Mora et al. 2022, Haque et al. 2024).

Usutu virus (USUV) is one of the mosquito-borne viruses emerging in Europe. This Orthoflavivirus, part of the Japanese encephalitis virus serocomplex, is maintained in an enzootic cycle primarily involving Culex mosquitoes as vectors and wild birds as amplifying hosts. In Europe, C. pipiens mosquitoes are the main vectors. Certain bird species, particularly the Eurasian Blackbird (Turdus merula), are highly susceptible to USUV and have experienced large-scale die-offs and population declines in USUV-affected regions (Lühken et al. 2017, Folly et al. 2020). Humans are incidental dead-end hosts, and most human infections are asymptomatic. However, symptomatic cases can present with fever and, occasionally, encephalitis or meningo-encephalitis (reviewed in Cadar and Simonin 2022).

USUV was first isolated in South Africa in 1959 (McIntosh 1985) and has subsequently been reported sporadically in several other African countries (Nikolay et al. 2011, Diagne et al. 2019). The virus was first detected in Europe in 2001, when it was identified as the causative agent of a mass bird mortality event in Austria (Weissenböck et al. 2002). Earlier presence was traced back to 1996 in Italy, through retrospective analyses of samples from dead wild birds (Weissenböck et al. 2013). The virus has since spread to most European countries (Barzon et al. 2023).

In the Netherlands, USUV was first detected in live free-ranging birds and caused massive die-offs of free-ranging and captive birds in summer 2016 (Rijks et al. 2016). In 2018, the virus was detected in a limited number of blood donors (Zaaijer et al. 2019). Epidemiological analyses showed that USUV caused outbreaks among wild birds between 2016 and 2018, followed by a period of lesser detection and a new surge in cases in 2022 (Münger et al. 2025). Initial genomic analyses provided evidence for the co-circulation in the Netherlands of genetically diverse USUV strains from two different lineages, Africa 3 and Europe 3 (Oude Munnink et al. 2020, Münger et al. 2025), showing that some of these strains likely emerged several years before their first detection in the country (Oude Munnink et al. 2020). These findings on USUV epidemiology stem from a nationwide, integrated sampling framework for arbovirus detection and monitoring, which combines sampling of live and dead free-ranging birds, captive birds, and mosquitoes (Münger et al. 2025). As part of this framework, USUV-positive samples have been consistently subjected to whole-genome sequencing, yielding a comprehensive genomic dataset that reflects USUV emergence and dynamics in wildlife of the Netherlands.

While USUV is not considered an important human health threat, its emergence in northwestern Europe has been pivotal in our understanding of the potential for mosquito-borne disease emergence beyond Mediterranean regions, in areas with temperate oceanic climates. USUV is phylogenetically closely related to other human-pathogenic arboviruses, such as West Nile virus, and has similar host and vector ranges (Nikolay 2015). Therefore, USUV circulation in a setting may be an indicator of increasingly suitable conditions for other arboviruses, which may follow similar transmission dynamics upon emergence.

In this study, we leverage USUV genomic monitoring by applying phylodynamic approaches to characterize USUV emergence, spread, and establishment in the Netherlands. We complemented and analysed a dataset of near-full-length USUV genome sequences from birds and mosquitoes of the Netherlands spanning 2016–23. Specifically, we aimed to (i) investigate the introduction(s) of USUV into the Netherlands, (ii) analyse the spatiotemporal dispersal of the main introduced lineages, and (iii) explore environmental factors that might have impacted this dispersal dynamic. In a context where arboviruses are expanding their geographic range across Europe, this study explores the dynamics of emergence and establishment of mosquito-borne viruses in a previously nonendemic temperate region.

Methods

Sampling of birds and mosquitoes

USUV samples were collected as part of an existing long-term research project on mosquito-borne viruses in birds and mosquitoes in the Netherlands initiated in 2016, as previously described (Münger et al. 2025). In short, from 2016 to 2023, dead free-ranging birds were reported through a citizen science system (Dutch Wildlife Health Centre (DWHC) 2024) and dead captive birds were submitted by zoos and veterinarians. Live, free-ranging birds were captured and sampled by trained volunteer ornithologists across the Netherlands. Systematic mosquito trapping was introduced in 2020 at a selection of bird-ringing sites across the Netherlands, by deploying fermenting molasses-baited traps one night per week from July to October every year (Krol et al. 2023). In addition to this continued monitoring, sequences from mosquito samples obtained during a study that collected samples at multiple sites in the Netherlands in the summers of 2016 and 2017 were included, as well as sequences from two smaller scale localized studies in the provinces of Utrecht, 2021 (Krol et al. 2024b), and Gelderland, 2022 (unpublished). For live birds and mosquitoes, sampling date was registered; for dead birds, date of death, date of finding, and/or date of sampling were registered. Geographic coordinates were recorded for all mosquito samples, all dead free-ranging birds, and most live birds. In cases where precise coordinates were unavailable for live birds, the location was geocoded to at least the municipality level. For dead captive birds, the institution where the bird died was recorded, allowing for geolocation.

USUV diagnostics and whole genome sequencing

Samples from dead birds (with brain tissue as preferred sample), live birds (throat and cloacal swabs), and mosquito pools were screened using real-time PCRs (RT-PCR) for the presence of USUV (Nikolay et al. 2014). Positive results were confirmed by a second USUV (Jöst et al. 2011) RT-PCR targeting a different region of the genome. Phocine distemper virus was added as an internal nucleic acid (NA) extraction control (van Doornum et al. 2007). USUV-positive samples with Ct values below 32 were submitted to whole genome sequencing using an USUV-targeted amplicon-based Oxford Nanopore approach as previously described (Oude Munnink et al. 2019). Reads were first mapped to the most similar reference genome from a selected reference set using Minimap2 (Li 2021). A consensus sequence was extracted, after which reads were remapped to this consensus, and a new consensus sequence was generated. Positions with coverage below 30 reads were replaced with ‘N’. Primer-binding regions were manually inspected and resolved by consulting raw reads and reference genomes. Only near-full-length USUV genome sequences (i.e. covering at least 80% of the viral genome) were retained for subsequent phylogenetic analyses.

Selection of publicly available USUV genome sequences

We retrieved all publicly available USUV genome sequences from GenBank on 5 May 2025, along with associated location and sampling date. To improve the precision of metadata used in downstream phylodynamic analyses, we conducted a bibliographic screening of publications associated with these sequences. Publicly available sequences were aligned with sequences from the Netherlands using MAFFT v7.487 (Katoh et al. 2002), and the resulting alignment was verified and edited using AliView (Larsson 2014). The length of the genome covered and the genomic regions targeted varied across studies (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To determine the sequences to include in subsequent phylodynamic investigations, we conducted several preliminary maximum-likelihood phylogenetic analyses using IQ-TREE v2.1.4-beta (Minh et al. 2020) on different subsets of the available genomic data. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using ModelFinder for model selection (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017), with ultrafast bootstrap support calculated from 1000 replicates (Hoang et al. 2018). We then explored the temporal structure of these subsets and identified outlier sequences that could impact the training of a molecular clock model using root-to-tip regression analyses conducted with the program TempEst (Rambaut et al. 2016) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Based on these preliminary analyses, two datasets were selected to maximize geographic coverage while obtaining sufficient temporal structure: (i) a dataset including near-full-length genomes and partial sequences of over 430 nucleotides covering the E gene, comprising 883 geo-referenced sequences (720 near-full-length genomes and 163 complete or partial E gene sequences; 585 sequences from global origins other than the Netherlands), hereafter referred to as the NFG + E dataset, as well as (ii) a dataset including near-full-length genomes and a group of partial sequences covering the NS5 gene, comprising 910 geo-referenced sequences (720 near-full-length genomes and 190 complete or partial NS5 gene sequences; 612 sequences from global origins other than the Netherlands), hereafter referred to as the NFG + NS5 dataset (Supplementary Fig. 1). Accession numbers and deposited metadata of publicly available USUV genome sequences included in our analyses are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

General methodology for phylodynamic analyses

All phylodynamic analyses described below were conducted using the software package BEAST v1.10 (Suchard et al. 2018), assessing the convergence and mixing of the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains using Tracer 1.7 (Rambaut et al. 2018), ensuring that the effective sample size (ESS) values for all continuous parameters were >200. Tracer was also used to determine the appropriate number of sampled trees to discard as burn-in. We also used the program TreeAnnotator 1.10.4 (Suchard et al. 2018) to retrieve and annotate a maximum clade credibility tree for each analysis.

Identifying clades circulating within the Netherlands

Discrete phylogeographic analyses of the NFG + E and NFG + NS5 datasets were conducted to identify USUV clades circulating within the Netherlands and that would correspond to distinct introduction events of a transmission chain into the region. We initially attempted to jointly infer a time-scaled phylogenetic tree and perform discrete phylogeographic analysis in BEAST v1.10 (Suchard et al. 2018). The substitution process was modelled using a GTR + Γ4 parametrisation (Tavaré 1986), as supported by model selection in IQ-TREE (Nguyen et al. 2015), while accommodating among-site rate heterogeneity. Branch-specific evolutionary rates were modelled according to a relaxed molecular clock with an underlying log-normal distribution (Drummond et al. 2006) to account for rate variation among lineages, and the flexible skygrid (Gill et al. 2013) coalescent model was specified as tree prior to allow for changes in effective population size. With these settings, we conducted a discrete phylogeographic analysis, using the Bayesian Stochastic Search Variable Selection (BSSVS) method (Kuo and Mallick 1998, Chipman et al. 2001, Lemey et al. 2009) while considering only two discrete locations: ‘the Netherlands’ and ‘other’. However, poor MCMC mixing indicated that these analyses would not achieve adequate posterior sampling within a reasonable timeframe. To circumvent this limitation, we first inferred a time-scaled phylogenetic tree using TreeTime v0.8.6 (Sagulenko et al. 2018) and then used the resulting time-scaled tree as a fixed tree topology in a subsequent discrete phylogeographic analysis conducted in BEAST v1.10.4 (Suchard et al. 2018) as described above. The analysis was run for 3 million MCMC iterations while sampling every 10 000 iterations. This analysis revealed a major clade circulating in the Netherlands, within which two well-supported phylogenetic clusters were identified. Given the sparse availability of USUV genomes from western Europe outside the Netherlands, we considered that the placement of the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of these two clusters within the Netherlands might be influenced by sampling bias. To avoid over-interpreting this ancestral placement, we treated the two clusters as independent clades in subsequent analyses (hereafter ‘Clade 1’ and ‘Clade 2’). Subsequent analyses focused on the near-full-length genome sequences sampled in the Netherlands composing these two dominant clades (Clade 1: n = 106; Clade 2: n = 154). The temporal signal of both clades was assessed by root-to-tip regression analyses conducted with the program TempEst (Rambaut et al. 2016) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Inferring the viral effective population size over time

We inferred changes in effective population size, Ne, associated with these main USUV clades using the flexible skygrid coalescent model (Gill et al. 2013) implemented in BEAST 1.10. For these analyses, the substitution process was modelled according to a GTR + Γ4 parametrization (Tavaré 1986), branch-specific evolutionary rates were modelled according to a relaxed molecular clock with an underlying log-normal distribution (Drummond et al. 2006), and changes in effective population size were allowed at the start of each meteorological season. Near-full-length genome sequences from the Netherlands composing Clade 1 and Clade 2 were analysed jointly, with both clades sharing substitution model parameters, but each with a distinct effective population size trajectory.

Under the skygrid model, the phylogenetic tree’s shape and branching times, which reflect the underlying genetic diversity, are used to infer temporal changes in the effective population size of an idealized population (e.g. lacking spatial structure). These temporal dynamics can be used to approximate changes in the size of the true viral population. However, the model assumes that sampling times are either fixed or independently distributed with respect to the effective population size. When there is preferential sampling, meaning that the sampling times are correlated with the effective population size, it can introduce systematic bias and result in misleading inferences. To guard against this, we also inferred the effective population size over time using the Bayesian nonparametric population reconstruction with preferential sampling (BNPR-PS) method developed by Karcher et al. (2020). The BNPR-PS framework accounts for preferential sampling by modelling the sampling intensity as a log-linear function of the log effective population size and, potentially, other time-varying covariates believed to be associated with the distribution of sampling times. Because USUV sequencing intensity in our study is also tied to the mortality of wild birds with USUV, we included the seasonal count of USUV-positive dead birds as a covariate, applying a (natural) log-transform:

Continuous phylogeographic reconstructions

To reconstruct the spread of the two main USUV clades circulating within the Netherlands, we performed a continuous phylogeographic analysis using the two-dimensional relaxed random walk (RRW) diffusion model (Lemey et al. 2010, Pybus et al. 2012) implemented in BEAST v1.10 (Suchard et al. 2018). This enabled reconstruction of the dispersal history of viral lineages in a continuous space while considering branch-specific variation in dispersal velocity. Because this method requires unique sampling coordinates assigned to each sample (i.e. the tips of the tree), we applied a ‘jitter’ for 63 of the sequences, adding random noise to the sampling coordinates drawn uniformly from a 2000 m2 window (estimated to fall within the range of Blackbird territory sizes (Snow 1956, Wysocki et al. 2004, van Irsel et al. 2026).

For this analysis, the other settings were specified as detailed above: a GTR + Γ4 substitution model, a log-normal relaxed molecular clock (Drummond et al. 2006), and a skygrid (Gill et al. 2013) tree prior with changes in effective population size allowed at the start of each meteorological season. Near-full-length genome sequences from the Netherlands composing Clade 1 and Clade 2 were analysed jointly, with both clades sharing substitution model and diffusion model parameters, but each with a separate effective population size trajectory via an independent skygrid coalescent model. The MCMC simulation was run for 2 billion iterations and sampled every 200 000 iterations.

We used the R package ‘seraphim’ (Dellicour et al. 2016a) to extract the spatio-temporal information embedded in 900 posterior trees, to visualize the continuous phylogeographic reconstruction obtained for each clade, and to estimate two lineage dispersal statistics: the weighted diffusion coefficient (Trovão et al. 2015) and isolation-by-distance signal (Dellicour et al. 2024) metrics. While the weighted diffusion coefficient measures the area that inferred lineages invade per unit of time (Trovão et al. 2015), the isolation-by-distance signal metric measures to what extent phylogenetic branches are spatially structured, that is, the tendency for phylogenetically closely related tip nodes to originate from locations that are geographically close (Dellicour et al. 2024).

Assessing the impact of the heterogeneous sampling effort within the study area

To assess the potential impact of heterogeneous sampling effort across the study area on the outcomes of our continuous phylogeographic analysis, we also conducted the continuous phylogeographic analyses on subsampled datasets where sequences were randomly subsampled per province and year according to USUV ecological suitability. To this end, we first quantified the intensity of USUV sequence sampling relative to modelled USUV occurrence probability from van Irsel et al. (2025): for each province and year, we calculated suitability values as the sum of raster cell probabilities within provincial boundaries (Supplementary Table 1). USUV sequences were grouped by province and year, and a sampling ratio (number of sequences per province-year divided by province-year suitability) was then calculated to use the resulting median ratio across province-years with nonzero sampling as a target. We then generated 10 unique subsampled sequence sets by randomly retaining sequences up to the target intensity for each province-year (see the Supplementary Methods for further details). It should be noted that, due to sparse sampling in some province-years, this procedure primarily reduced oversampling rather than achieved full homogenization. Each subsampled dataset was analysed under the same continuous phylogeographic framework as the full dataset, and dispersal statistics were computed to evaluate the robustness to sampling heterogeneity of phylogeographic inferences. The 10 subsampled datasets were also used in analyses assessing the impact of environmental factors on the dispersal locations of viral lineages (see below).

Discrete phylogeographic reconstructions

We further conducted discrete phylogeographic analyses to investigate the frequency of viral movement among the 12 provinces of the Netherlands and identify major patterns of viral spread. Analyses were based on 1000 empirical trees treated as fixed topologies and sampled from the skygrid time-scaled phylogenetic inference analysis (as described in Inferring the Viral Effective Population Size over Time subsection). Specifically, we used the Bayesian Stochastic Search Variable Selection (BSSVS) (Kuo and Mallick 1998, Chipman et al. 2001, Lemey et al. 2009) procedure implemented in BEAST 1.10 to identify and quantify the support for the transition events between the sampled provinces. To assess the statistical support for each transition event, we calculated and reported their Bayes Factor (BF) (Jeffreys and John 1935) and adjusted BF (Chaillon et al. 2020, Vrancken et al. 2020, Gámbaro et al. 2025), the latter incorporating the relative abundance of the involved trait states (i.e. sampling intensity). The MCMC was run for 1 billion iterations while sampling posterior trees every 100 000 iterations.

Landscape phylogeographic analyses

We conducted previously introduced (Dellicour et al. 2016b, Dellicour et al. 2020) landscape phylogeographic analyses to investigate potential associations between environmental factors and (i) the dispersal location (Dellicour et al. 2019) and (ii) the diffusion velocity (Dellicour et al. 2017, 2025) of viral lineages. Both approaches, implemented in the R package ‘seraphim’ 2.0 (Dellicour et al. 2016a, Dellicour et al. 2026), generate a null dispersal model, consisting of the inferred posterior trees whose branch positions were randomized within the study area (defined as the minimum convex hull polygon around all the tree node locations), while their topology and the inferred root location were maintained (hereafter referred to as randomized trees). Each inferred tree is paired with a corresponding randomized tree that differs only in the geographic coordinates of its nodes (except for the root node location), while all other phylogenetic properties remained unchanged (Dellicour et al. 2016b).

We used a first landscape phylogeographic approach to investigate the impact of environmental factors on the dispersal location of viral lineages (Dellicour et al. 2019, 2020). Specifically, we extracted environmental raster values at the node positions of 100 inferred and corresponding randomized trees. For each considered environmental raster, we then obtained two posterior distributions of mean environmental values: one for the inferred trees and one for the corresponding randomized trees. The support for a nonrandom spatial association between environmental values and tree node locations was approximated using Bayes factors based on the comparison between these two posterior distributions. These analyses were conducted on the full dataset as well as on the 10 subsampled datasets (see above), and each environmental factor was once tested as an environmental variable potentially attracting or repelling viral lineages, i.e. testing whether environmental values at inferred node locations tended to be respectively higher or lower than those at randomized node positions under the null model.

We used a second landscape phylogeographic approach to investigate whether viral lineage diffusion velocity was impacted by environmental factors acting as potential conductance or resistance factors. For each environmental raster under investigation, environmental distances were computed along phylogenetic branches using two different path models: the least-cost (Dijkstra 1959) and Circuitscape (McRae 2006) path models. These models approximate the spatial path linking the start and end locations of each phylogenetic branch and compute, for each branch, an environmental distance based on the raster cells encountered along this path. Squared environmental distances were then correlated to branch dispersal durations to test whether a proportion of the heterogeneity in branch diffusion coefficients could be explained by the spatial heterogeneity of an environmental raster (Dellicour et al. 2025). Detailed descriptions of raster transformations and statistical procedures are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Environmental factors included two climate variables derived from the CHELSA v2.1 dataset (Karger et al. 2017, 2023), six land cover categories derived from the CorineLandCover inventory (European Environment Agency (EEA) 2020), and log-transformed human population density (Supplementary Table 2). As climate variables, the number of days with daily minimum temperature >10°C (growing degree days) was included as a proxy for the length of the mosquito activity season (Becker et al. 2020) and minimum winter temperature as a proxy for winter severity, which may influence ecological aspects as mosquito survival during diapause or bird movement (Bonnet-Lebrun et al. 2020, Rozsypal et al. 2021). Land cover categories were selected based on their prevalence in northwestern Europe and their potential relevance to arbovirus circulation (Supplementary Table 3). Human population density was included as it may represent both an ecological factor for synanthropic animal species and as a proxy for sampling bias, since a substantial fraction of our data originated from citizen-science reporting.

Results

USUV genome sequencing and dataset composition

A total of 298 near-full-length genome sequences sampled between 2016 and 2023 from birds and mosquitoes from the Netherlands were analysed in this study, including 48 newly generated sequences (Oude Munnink et al. 2020, Münger et al. 2025) (Supplementary Table 4). Most genomic sequences were derived from dead free-ranging birds (68%, 202 sequences), while 38 sequences were derived from live free-ranging birds (13%), 27 from dead captive birds (9%), and 31 from mosquito pools (10%). Eurasian Blackbirds were the predominant host species from which USUV genome sequences were obtained, representing 92% of dead free-ranging birds (186/202) and 82% of live free-ranging birds (31/38), reflecting the species’ high representation among USUV-positive detections in epidemiological surveillance (Münger et al. 2025). The predominance of sequences derived from citizen science-reported dead birds may preferentially represent viral circulation in highly populated and accessible areas.

Introductions and persistence dynamics of USUV in the Netherlands

Discrete phylogeographic reconstruction based on the global USUV dataset (NFG + E and NFG + NS5) yielded concordant results across datasets, inferring seven distinct USUV introduction events into the Netherlands (Fig. 1). One of these introduction events gave rise to a large clade belonging to the Africa 3 lineage and encompassing most sequences from the Netherlands (260/298, 87.2%). The remaining six introductions gave rise to clades comprising one additional Africa 3 sequence and five smaller clades belonging to the Europe 3 lineage.

Within the main Africa 3 clade, sequences from the Netherlands formed two well-supported phylogenetic clusters (bootstrap ≥90 across both datasets). Given the limited availability of USUV genomes from western Europe outside the Netherlands, the inferred MRCA of these clusters within the country may be inaccurately placed. To account for the uncertainty of this inference, we treated these two clusters as independent clades in subsequent analyses (hereafter ‘Clade 1’ and ‘Clade 2’). The following results focus on these two dominant clades having circulated within the Netherlands.

Population dynamics of major USUV clades circulating in the Netherlands

Clade 1 was frequently detected in the Netherlands from 2016 to 2018, after which detections declined sharply. Our time-scaled phylogenetic inference estimated the TMRCA of this clade to be around 2011 (95% highest posterior density [HPD]: 2009–13; Fig. 2a). Ne reconstruction through time [Ne(t)] indicated exponential-like growth until around 2017 to early 2018, followed by stabilization (Fig. 2b). Clade 2 was detected concurrently with Clade 1 from 2016 to 2018, with fewer detections in 2019–21, and was again frequently detected in 2022 and 2023. The TMRCA of this clade was estimated to be around 2012 (95% HPD: 2010–13). Estimated Ne(t) through time again showed an exponential-like growth until around 2016–17 followed by an apparent stabilization.

To account for potential biases associated with increased sampling during the USUV activity period, we applied the BNPR-PS model, which explicitly models the sampling process, and included the log-transformed seasonal counts of USUV-positive dead birds as a covariate. For both clades, the BNPR-PS demographic reconstructions were compatible with and more precise than the skygrid demographic reconstructions (Fig. 2b). In particular, for each clade, the median log effective population size trajectory inferred under the BNPR-PS model was contained within the 95% HPD interval region inferred under the skygrid, and the 95% HPD interval region for most of the demographic history. Unlike the skygrid model, the BNPR-PS framework accounts for variation in detection through time by explicitly modelling the sampling process (see the Methods section for further details). For both clades, the coefficient for the log effective population size (β1) was positive (95% HPD excluding zero), indicating that periods of higher detection were associated with larger Ne(t) (Supplementary Fig. 4). The coefficient for the dead-bird covariate (β2) differed between clades: it was positive for Clade 2 but negative for Clade 1, with 95% HPD intervals excluding zero for both clades. The positive association in Clade 2 suggests that higher numbers of USUV-associated dead birds were linked to increased detection while controlling for Ne(t). The negative association in Clade 1 likely reflects that Ne(t) was already declining during the 2018 mortality peak and that this clade was only rarely detected during subsequent mortality events in 2022–23.

Dispersal dynamics of major USUV clades circulating in the Netherlands

Continuous phylogeographic reconstructions for both clades indicated that, prior to the detection of the 2016 outbreak, USUV was likely circulating in the central-eastern region of the country (Fig. 3).

Clade 1 primarily circulated in the central region in 2016 and, to a lesser extent, in the south. From 2017, and more markedly in 2018, USUV spread northwards. From 2019 onwards, there was only limited circulation of this clade. Clade 2 circulated mainly in the central region in 2016, with some dispersal to the north. In 2017 and 2018, circulation persisted in the centre and became more pronounced in the north. Unlike Clade 1, Clade 2 continued to circulate after 2018, mainly in the central-southern region. In 2022 and 2023, circulation intensified, with an extended range across the central and southern regions and sporadic circulation detections in the north. Across both clades, continuous phylogeographic reconstructions showed an overall star-like pattern, with most tips at the country’s periphery that seemed to radiate from a central ancestral location. While the central locations inferred for these internal nodes could, at least to some extent, arise from a potential phylogeographic artefact, such a star-like pattern reveals low phylogeographic structure inherent to circulating clades. In other words, this phylogeographic pattern highlights a notable intermixing of USUV lineages circulating within the country. Nevertheless, such star-like patterns may be (partially) driven by sampling geometry and heterogeneous regional sampling density; we therefore conducted subsampling analyses to test the robustness of these observations (see below).

We used the continuous phylogeographic reconstructions to calculate spread statistics jointly for both clades. The weighted diffusion coefficient (Trovão et al. 2015) of the USUV clades within the Netherlands was estimated at 285 km2/year (95% HPD: 240–330). The isolation-by-distance signal (Dellicour et al. 2024) was estimated at 0.07 (95% HPD: 0.06–0.09), indicating a very weak correlation between geographic proximity and phylogenetic relatedness, which is in line with the substantial intermixing pattern highlighted by the respective phylogeographic reconstructions.

Discrete phylogeographic reconstructions indicated that both clades were predominantly maintained within the central provinces of Gelderland and Utrecht, with Clade 2 additionally showing a strong association with North Brabant (Fig. 4). As supported by the adjusted Bayes factor (Gámbaro et al. 2025), Clade 1 spread mainly from Gelderland (central-east) towards northern and southern provinces and westwards via Utrecht while Clade 2 showed bidirectional transitions between Gelderland and North Brabant, with additional transitions from these central provinces towards the country’s periphery, via Overijssel northwards and via Utrecht westwards. Overall, these lineage transition events echo the star-like pattern observed in the continuous phylogeographic analysis, with viral lineages radiating outward from central provinces, and, for Clade 2, from a southern province, towards the country’s periphery.

Because USUV sampling was uneven across the Netherlands, with central provinces (Utrecht, Gelderland, and Flevoland) being oversampled relative to peripheral provinces (Supplementary Fig. 5), we used subsampled datasets to assess the potential impact of this heterogeneous sampling effort on the continuous phylogeographic pattern reported above. Continuous phylogeographic reconstructions based on 10 randomly subsampled datasets—with sampling weighted by the estimated probability of virus occurrence per province per year—revealed dispersal patterns similar to those inferred based on the full dataset, consistently displaying a star-like pattern (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Weighted diffusion coefficients and isolation-by-distance estimates showed no systematic deviations; when significant differences were detected, values remained within the range inferred from the full dataset (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Based on these findings, we retained the full dataset for subsequent analyses of environmental predictors of lineage diffusion velocity while explicitly testing for potential effects on dispersal location using the subsampled datasets, as these analyses are particularly sensitive to the geographic distribution of sampled sequences (Dellicour et al. 2020).

Testing the impact of environmental factors on the dispersal dynamics of USUV lineages

We investigated potential associations between nine environmental factors and USUV lineage dispersal location and diffusion velocity (Fig. 5). Analyses assessing the association between dispersal locations of USUV lineages and environmental conditions indicated a positive association with urban areas associated with relatively higher human population density; this association may, however, be driven by increased detection and reporting of dead birds in relatively densely populated areas. In contrast, our results indicate that USUV lineages did not tend to circulate in areas associated with relatively high agricultural land cover (Supplementary Table 5). As for our investigation of associations between environmental factors and the diffusion velocity of inferred USUV lineages, we found no support for an association between the heterogeneity in lineage diffusion velocity and the tested environmental factors.

Discussion

Our phylodynamic and phylogeographic analyses of USUV emergence and persistence dynamics in the Netherlands region reveal multiple introductions of USUV lineages Europe 3 and Africa 3 into this region, leading to the local establishment of lineage Africa 3. These results substantiate earlier hypotheses from our smaller-scale phylogenetic studies and provide a refined picture consistent with previous nonphylodynamic evidence (Oude Munnink et al. 2020, Münger et al. 2025). While our analyses identify seven independent introductions of USUV into the Netherlands region, this number is likely underestimated as a consequence of limited genomic monitoring of USUV in surrounding northwestern European areas. Notably, in Germany, where USUV was first detected in the southwest in 2011 (Ziegler et al. 2015), bird cases increased in 2016 as the virus spread to new areas, including to regions bordering the Netherlands (Bergmann et al. 2023). Genomic monitoring has been ongoing in Germany since 2011 and shows a shift from the predominance of lineage Europe 3 to Africa 3 around 2019 (Bergmann et al. 2023). However, the number of available sequences from 2016 to 2018 is low relative to the Netherlands, particularly given the substantial USUV circulation in Germany (e.g. 1 208 USUV-positive free-ranging and captive birds in 2018 versus 119 in the Netherlands). Circulation of lineage Africa 3 has also been reported in Belgium (Benzarti et al. 2020) and in France (Decors et al. 2019) in the years corresponding to the first detected outbreaks in the Netherlands (2016–18), but very few genome sequences are available from these countries. Consequently, the apparent clustering of sequences from the Netherlands and the inferred MRCA locations may, in part, reflect gaps in regional sampling rather than the true geographic origin of these introductions. The number and timing of inferred introductions into the Netherlands region are thus conditional on current sampling coverage and may change if genomic monitoring increases in other countries or if archived samples from across northwestern Europe are retrospectively sequenced. One of the seven inferred introductions corresponds to a major USUV lineage Africa 3 clade that accounts for the majority of sequences detected in the Netherlands. Aware of the uncertainty surrounding the placement of the MRCA of this clade, and because earlier analyses supported two distinct phylogenetic clusters within it (Oude Munnink et al. 2020), we treated this large clade as two separate introductions in downstream analyses.

Our results suggest a parallel emergence of the two main USUV clades circulating in the Netherlands several years before the first documented USUV outbreak in 2016 and the associated bird mortality (Rijks et al. 2016), indicating at least 3 years of cryptic circulation. Both clades comprised sampled sequences and inferred ancestral nodes spanning multiple years, supporting a long-term persistence and overwintering hypothesis of USUV in the Netherlands or at least in the broader northwestern European region. Our analyses indicate that both clades showed rapid growth in effective population size until around 2016–18, followed by a period of relative stability, consistent with an initial invasion phase followed by local establishment. Several mechanisms have been proposed for the overwintering of USUV in northwestern Europe (Blom et al. 2022), including infected diapausing mosquitoes (Sauer et al. 2023, Koenraadt et al. 2024), vertical transmission in mosquitoes (Schilling et al. 2025), and persistent infections in avian hosts (Kuno 2001, Schmidt et al. 2021, Münger et al. 2025).

Earlier epidemiological evidence showed that USUV outbreaks among wild birds in the Netherlands region occurred from 2016 to 2018, inclusive, followed by a sharp decline in detections and a new surge in cases in 2022 (Münger et al. 2025). The intervening ‘quiet phase’ was observed not only in the reduced number of USUV-positive dead birds (the primary source of genomic data) but also in lower prevalence among live-sampled birds. This supports a genuine reduction in viral circulation, at least in the species best captured by surveillance, the Eurasian Blackbird. However, while detections of Clade 1 decreased sharply after 2018, the inferred effective population size did not collapse. This inference and the more recent incidental detections of Clade 1 might reflect continued low-level circulation in the Netherlands region, or introductions from an unsampled population where this clade continues to circulate, such as a region outside the Netherlands or a poorly sampled host community. In contrast, Clade 2 persisted locally during the epidemiological ‘quiet phase’ and was associated with the renewed surge in USUV cases from 2022 onwards, indicating local persistence for approximately a decade.

The preliminary discrete phylogeographic reconstruction conducted to delimit clades that could correspond to distinct introduction events into the Netherlands region is consistent with the hypothesis of a persisting USUV circulation across multiple years in this region. Our subsequent continuous phylogeographic reconstructions suggest that early circulation (prior to 2016) of USUV from both major Dutch clades occurred in the central-eastern Netherlands, within the Rhine Delta region near the German border. This result could, however, arise from a phylogeographic artefact, as these locations and associated uncertainty also correspond to the central area relative to the sampling distribution: given the noninclusion in those analyses of geo-referenced viral genomes from outside the Netherlands, continuous phylogeographic analyses will have more propensity to infer the root node locations in the middle of all sampling locations.

Our continuous and discrete phylogeographic analyses reveal a star-like dispersal pattern for the two main circulating clades detected in the region, with inferred lineage dispersal events frequently radiating from the centre of the study area towards more peripheral locations. Yet, as introduced above, this star-like diffusion pattern could to some extent result from lower availability of sequences from peripheral regions within the Netherlands and areas surrounding the country. To account for the sampling effort heterogeneity, we (i) performed the continuous phylogeographic analysis using 10 subsampled datasets and (ii) reported the results of the discrete phylogeographic analysis using an adjusted Bayes factor accounting for sampling heterogeneity. The consistent recovery of this star-like dispersal pattern highlights an important intermixing dynamic and a limited phylogeographic structure within the study area. In other words, two positive cases detected in close vicinity have a relatively low probability to be closely connected within the transmission chain, as also reflected by the weak isolation-by-distance signal estimated for those clades. While direct comparisons to other studies are challenging, WNV also appears to show substantial intermixing in eastern Germany (a region approximately two times the size of the Netherlands) (Ziegler et al. 2020, Patzina-Mehling et al. 2025), as does USUV in Veneto, northeastern Italy (approximately half the size of the Netherlands) (Zecchin et al. 2021). For both USUV and WNV, some phylogeographic structure exists at the continental scale in Europe (Engel et al. 2016, Lu et al. 2024), while substantial intermixing appears to occur at smaller scales.

The intermixing of USUV lineages within the Netherlands could reflect circulation driven by stochastic movements of amplifying hosts over relatively long distances. The main vector of USUV in Europe, C. pipiens, typically disperses only short distances, usually on the order of hundreds of metres to a few kilometres (Ciota et al. 2012, Hamer et al. 2014, Verdonschot and Besse-Lototskaya 2014). Eurasian Blackbirds, the most severely affected bird species during USUV outbreaks, generally exhibit small movement ranges as well, although some migratory and partially migratory populations exist (van Vliet et al. 2009, de Wit et al. 2026). Evidence of exposure to or infection with USUV has been found in numerous bird species, which might contribute in different ways to its reservoir system (Meister et al. 2008, Nikolay 2015, Münger et al. 2025, de Wit et al. 2026). We therefore speculate that this intermixing pattern could be consistent with the ‘silent reservoir’ hypothesis proposed by a previous modelling study of the Dutch USUV outbreak (de Wit et al. 2026), which suggested that the observed epidemiological patterns could not be explained by USUV persistence in Eurasian Blackbirds alone. That study indicated that additional bird species with lower infection-associated mortality, longer lifespans, and greater dispersal capacity likely act as critical silent reservoirs sustaining transmission; high immunity within this reservoir subsequently helped reduce the effective reproduction number after the 2016–18 outbreaks. Such a mechanism is also compatible with our phylodynamic inference for Clade 2, where effective population size stabilized rather than collapsed during the subsequent epidemiological ‘quiet phase’.

Our analyses of potential associations between environmental factors and viral lineage dispersal location (Dellicour et al. 2019) indicate a positive association with higher urbanization and higher human population density and a negative association with agricultural areas. An important caveat is that, as a substantial fraction of our data originated from citizen-science reporting of dead birds, the observed associations may be influenced by increased detection and reporting in densely populated areas rather than reflecting true differences in transmission dynamics. Nonetheless, a genuine ecological association with urbanization is plausible and consistent with the ecology of Eurasian Blackbirds, which are most abundant in urban settings in the Netherlands (Dellar et al. 2025). Culex pipiens are widespread and commonly found across habitats in the Netherlands (Ibañez-Justicia et al. 2015) and abundant in urban areas (Krol et al. 2024a), where higher temperatures may favour longer mosquito activity seasons and enhance viral replication through the urban heat island effect (Townroe and Callaghan 2014, Fyie et al. 2023). Recent ecological work also suggests that, beyond Eurasian Blackbirds, USUV outbreaks in the Netherlands have also negatively affected populations of other urban bird species, consistent with a broader urban-associated transmission dynamic (van Irsel 2026). A Europe-wide phylodynamic analysis of West Nile virus lineage 2 (Lu et al. 2024), a flavivirus closely related to USUV, similarly reported an association between dispersal locations of viral lineages and urbanized areas. The associations tested here are directly based on environmental values extracted at internal and tip node positions, making their outcome particularly sensitive to the geographic distribution of sampled sequences (Dellicour et al. 2020). We partially addressed this by repeating analyses on 10 subsampled datasets to reduce spatial sampling bias; results should nonetheless be interpreted with consideration of these methodological dependencies.

In conclusion, our phylodynamic analyses of USUV emergence and persistence in the Netherlands support the hypothesis that temperate regions of northwestern Europe can sustain long-term persistence and overwintering of this mosquito-borne virus, and likely of related orthoflaviviruses. The patterns we observe, multiple independent introductions, local establishment, and extensive spatial intermixing provide a framework for understanding how closely related mosquito-borne viruses such as West Nile virus might emerge, establish, and circulate in similar settings. While our detected associations with urban areas and higher population density may reflect reporting bias, they nonetheless highlight the existence of a zoonotic risk at the human–wildlife interface. Our contextualisation further underscores that these findings are inherently constrained by the availability of publicly accessible genomic data and that extracting the full potential of phylodynamic analyses requires coordinated regional surveillance and collaborative genomic monitoring of emerging mosquito-borne viruses across Europe. As environmental conditions are becoming increasingly favourable for mosquito-borne virus establishment in temperate regions of Europe, USUV establishment in the Netherlands region serves as an early warning for possible establishment of other mosquito-borne viruses and should catalyse the development of preparedness strategies, from optimized surveillance and response systems to pre-emptive mitigation measures addressing vulnerability factors.
