Background:

West Nile virus (WNV) has recently been described as a possible trigger of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). There is little data in the literature about this association.

Subjects and Methods:

In the present meta-summary, eight case reports describing patients who experienced CVDs following a WNV infection were included. Patient characteristics, triggers, and type of syncope were collected.

Results:

A total of eight patients with a median age of 67.29 (interquartile range: 53–70) years were evaluated; 87.5% were male. Seventy-five percent of patients had an altered mental status. Left ventricular ejection fraction was impaired in five patients (62.5%). Four of eight patients (50%) had arrhythmias, with 3 of them having a normal ejection fraction. Sudden cardiac death was reported in one patient (12.5%).

Conclusions:

CVDs due to WNV have been associated with myocarditis and arrhythmias, among elderly patients, males, and those with neurological impairment.

INTRODUCTION

The West Nile virus (WNV) is a widespread mosquito-borne flavivirus that poses a significant public health issue in endemic regions.[1] Recently, the incidence of WNV infection has been steadily increasing in some Italian areas over the last years, particularly in the North-East,[23] and a new spread in Southern regions in 2025 is driving a national spike of cases.[4]

In a small fraction of cases (<1%), the virus invades the central nervous system, leading to a serious condition known as West Nile neuroinvasive disease.[5] This can manifest as encephalitis (50%–71%), meningitis (15%–35%), or acute flaccid paralysis (3%–19%).

For patients with the neuroinvasive form of WNV, long-term physical and cognitive issues can persist even after the initial symptoms disappear. These lasting problems may include fatigue, ongoing weaknesses, memory loss, depression, and difficulty concentrating. The prognosis for WNV infection depends on the form of the disease. While West Nile Fever and WNV meningitis typically have a good outcome, WNV encephalitis (WNE) is more severe. It carries a higher risk of death, with a fatality rate between 10% and 30%.[6]

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of morbidity and mortality among older adults, placing a significant burden on healthcare systems.[7] Although rare, recent case reports and studies have highlighted the potential for WNV infection to involve the cardiovascular system, leading to complications such as myocarditis, cardiomyopathy, and arrhythmias. This underscores the need for clinicians to be aware of this rare complication in patients with WNV.[89101112131415] Our meta-summary of case reports aimed to characterize patients who experienced CVDs during WNV infection.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

To ensure transparency and methodological rigor, this meta-summary was conducted following the principles outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guideline.[16]

Search strategy and study selection

The methodology involved a comprehensive search of three major electronic databases: PubMed, Google Scholar, and EMBASE. The search, which was conducted for the period from 2009 to 2023, utilized the following keywords: “West Nile Virus,” “Myocarditis,” and “Cardiovascular disease.” We focused on original reports published in English. Duplicate papers were systematically removed. The initial screening of all titles and abstracts was performed independently by two researchers (AM and VR). Any discrepancies in study selection were resolved through discussion and a final consensus. Ultimately, eight case reports/case series met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-summary. The detailed process of study selection is illustrated in Figure 1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included in this meta-summary if they met all of the following criteria:

Study Design: The study was a case report or a case series

Population: The study described one or more patients with a confirmed WNV infection

Exposure: The study reported on the development of a cardiovascular complication during or following the WNV infection

Outcomes: The study described the clinical outcome of the patient(s).

Studies were excluded for the following reasons: (i) they were review articles, editorials, or letters to the editor without original patient data; (ii) they were not published in English; (iii) they presented pooled data from which individual patient information could not be extracted; or (iv) WNV infection was suspected but not diagnostically confirmed.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (AM and VR) independently extracted relevant information from the included studies using a standardized, predefined data extraction form created in Microsoft Excel. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The following data were collected from each case:

Publication Details: First author, year of publication

Patient Demographics: Age and gender

Clinical Information: Preexisting comorbidities, baseline neurological status (e.g., normal vs. altered mental status)

Cardiovascular Findings: Type of cardiovascular complication, diagnostic evidence of myocarditis, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), presence and type of arrhythmias, length of hospital stays, and timing of complication onset

Prognosis: Clinical outcome (e.g., survival, death, persistence of cardiac impairment).

Data synthesis

Given the nature of the included studies (case reports/series without a comparator group), a formal meta-analysis to calculate relative risks or odds ratios was not feasible. Instead, a qualitative synthesis and meta-summary of the findings were performed. The extracted data were tabulated to provide a comprehensive overview of the patient population, clinical characteristics, cardiovascular manifestations, and outcomes.

RESULTS

The meta-summary included eight patients who developed cardiovascular complications following a WNV infection. The cohort had a median age of 67.29 years (interquartile range: 53–70), with a strong male predominance (87.5%). In addition to cardiac involvement, three-quarters of the patients (75%) exhibited neurological symptoms, including altered mental status. Preexisting comorbidities were present in 62.5% (5 of 8) of the patients. A majority of the patients (5/8, 62.5%) presented with an impaired LVEF. Arrhythmias were reported in half of the patients (4/8), with three of these cases occurring in patients with a normal ejection fraction. It is notable that the sole female patient was the only one who did not recover her ejection fraction and ultimately required a cardiac resynchronization therapy device. Sudden cardiac death was a reported outcome in one patient (12.5%). Data on the length of hospital stay and the timing of cardiac complications were not consistently available across all reports included.

DISCUSSION

Reports of WNV-associated cardiac involvement are currently rare: clinical attention during outbreaks often focuses on neurological complications, making cardiac involvement possibly underdiagnosed. Moreover, seasonal and epidemic patterns of WNV circulation may further limit opportunities for systematic cardiac evaluation. Although medical literature consistently identifies CVD, diabetes, hypertension, and immunosuppression as significant risk factors for severe outcomes, including cardiac manifestations,[1819] only 50% of the patients in this cohort had one of these preexisting conditions.

Cardiac arrhythmias are a frequent complication in critically ill patients. However, WNV type-specific arrhythmias have not been reported. Bode et al.[20] in their retrospective study, they enrolled 228 patients with WNV. Patients with WN were more likely to develop arrhythmia than were other patients; of 65 patients with WNE, 7 (11%) developed arrhythmia other than sinus bradycardia or tachycardia, compared with 2 (2%) of 103 patients who had WNV meningitis. Animal models suggest that this may result from viral infection of neurons within the cardiac conduction system leading to autonomic instability and subsequent arrhythmia.[21]

In the cases we described, patients developed bradyarrhythmia, which in one case was a complication of myocarditis and led to the patient’s death.

While serology and polymerase chain reaction can confirm WNV infection systemically, they do not reliably establish direct myocardial involvement. Endomyocardial biopsy, the gold standard for confirmation, is rarely feasible in critically ill patients, and postmortem examination remains the most reliable method for demonstrating myocardial necrosis with inflammatory infiltrates.[22] A previously reported case by Kushawaha et al.[10] linked a neuroinvasive disease with cardiac involvement, thanks to the autopsy finding of myocarditis, including scarring of the left ventricle and inflammatory cell infiltration. However, it remains uncertain whether the heart damage in WNV-associated myocarditis is caused by an indirect immune-mediated damage triggered by the virus or from direct viral invasion of the cardiac tissue.

A potential explanation for the direct viral involvement of the myocardium is the intrinsic cellular tropism and enhanced invasiveness of the WNV. This heightened viral aggressiveness may also underpin the concurrent neurological and cardiac manifestations observed in these patients. The review further suggests that advanced age and male sex are significant, commonly identified risk factors for the development of these WNV-induced cardiovascular complications. Notably, the elderly, along with severely immunosuppressed people, represent the category at highest risk of neurological complications and unfavorable prognosis.[22]

The present meta-summary, while the first to explore the association between WNV and CVDs, has some notable limitations. The most significant constraints are the exclusion of non-English language papers and the small number of included cases, which may affect the generalizability of the findings.

Despite these limitations, the analysis identified several common factors among the patients examined. Specifically, a consistent pattern emerged in which patients with WNV-related CVDs were frequently male, of advanced age, and had concurrent neurological involvement. While a definitive causal link between WNV and CVDs is challenging to establish from the available case reports, these shared patient characteristics are a key finding of the study.

CONCLUSIONS

This meta-summary, the first to explore the association between the WNV and CVDs, analyzed 8 cases of patients who developed cardiac complications following WNV infection. The findings indicate that these complications were found predominantly in male patients (87.5%), of advanced age (median age of 67.29 years), and with concurrent neurological involvement. A significant association was noted, with 75% of the patients also exhibiting neurological symptoms, including altered mental status. The most common cardiac complications were myocarditis and arrhythmias, with the latter present in 50% of the patients. An impaired LVEF was found in 62.5% of the cases. Larger studies are needed to better assess the incidence of cardiovascular complications in patients with WNV infections, as well as their precipitating factors and outcomes.
